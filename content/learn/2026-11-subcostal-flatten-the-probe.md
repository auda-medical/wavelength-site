The subcostal view is the window you need most in the sickest patients: during cardiac arrest, in the ventilated patient, and when the chest is hard to scan. It is also the view new scanners find hardest. The reason is nearly always the angle of the probe, not the patient.

![Wavelength Pearl: subcostal view. The probe laid almost flat below the xiphoid with an overhand grip, bowel gas with an upright probe, the subcostal four-chamber view through the liver, and the pericardium checked for fluid.](/learn/images/subcostal-flat-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## Before

Holding the probe like a pen, pointing straight into the abdomen, sends the beam back towards the spine. You see liver near the probe, then bowel gas with a grey, dirty shadow beneath it. The heart sits above and behind the ribs, out of the beam.

## The move

1. Ask the patient to lie flat and bend the knees. This relaxes the abdominal wall.
2. Use the phased array probe with the marker to the patient's left.
3. Change your grip: put your hand on top of the probe, palm down, so you hold it from above.
4. Place the probe just below the xiphoid, slightly to the patient's right, so the liver becomes your window.
5. Lay the probe almost flat against the skin and aim towards the left shoulder.
6. Increase the depth, then ask the patient to take a deep breath in and hold it. This brings the heart down towards the probe.

## After

The liver now fills the near field, with the heart behind it. The right ventricle sits nearest the probe, against the liver, with the left ventricle beyond it. The atria lie to the left of the screen and the apex points to the right. The pericardium is the bright line around the heart, and this is where you look for a pericardial effusion.

> **The aha** Flatter is better. If you see bowel gas, do not press harder. Drop your hand towards the patient's abdomen until the probe is almost flat.

> **Pitfall** Pressing hard below the ribs hurts and makes the patient tense the abdomen. Gentle pressure, the right angle and a held breath give a better window. In cardiac arrest, record a short clip and keep your scan within the pulse check.

+ The probe is too upright, so the beam goes back towards the spine
- The patient has a pericardial effusion
- The depth is too great
- The wrong probe is in use
: The heart sits above and behind the ribs. An upright probe sends the beam under it.

?? How should you hold the probe for the subcostal view?
+ Hand on top, palm down, with the probe almost flat and aimed towards the left shoulder
- Like a pen, pointing straight into the abdomen
- Upright, aimed towards the right hip
- Along the ribs, aimed at the spine
: The overhand grip lets you lay the probe flat, so the beam passes under the ribs.

?? In the subcostal four-chamber view, which chamber lies nearest the probe?
+ The right ventricle
- The left ventricle
- The left atrium
- The aorta
: The liver fills the near field, the right ventricle lies against it and the left ventricle lies beyond.

?? The window is still poor. Which step helps most?
+ Bend the patient's knees and ask for a deep breath in, held
- Press harder below the ribs
- Reduce the depth
- Sit the patient bolt upright
: Bent knees relax the abdominal wall, and a held breath brings the heart down towards the probe.

?? You use the subcostal view during cardiac arrest. What keeps the scan safe?
+ Record a short clip and keep the scan within the pulse check
- Scan during compressions until the image is perfect
- Pause compressions until you find the view
- Leave the probe on the chest throughout
: Review the clip after compressions restart, so the scan never prolongs the pause.
