A patient who cannot pass urine, a confused older person with lower abdominal pain, or a catheter that seems not to drain: each raises one question, is the bladder full? A bedside scan answers it in a minute. The kidneys answer the next question, how far back the obstruction reaches.

![Wavelength Pearl: urinary retention. Measure the bladder in transverse and sagittal, estimate the volume, then scan both kidneys.](/learn/images/bladder-retention-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Place the curvilinear probe just above the pubic symphysis, transverse, angled down into the pelvis.
2. Find the widest transverse view. Measure the width, side to side, and the depth, front to back.
3. Rotate to sagittal, marker to the head. Measure the length, head to foot.
4. Estimate the volume: length × width × depth × 0.52.
5. Scan both kidneys for hydronephrosis.
6. After catheterisation, rescan the bladder to confirm it has emptied.

## Reading the view

- **Retention:** a large, round, thin-walled bladder rising out of the pelvis. A volume of several hundred millilitres in a patient who cannot void supports the diagnosis. Record the number and compare it with the volume drained.
- **Upstream effect:** hydronephrosis on both sides with a large bladder suggests longstanding or high-pressure obstruction. Check the creatinine.
- **Catheter in place but bladder full:** the catheter is blocked or misplaced. Look for the balloon. A balloon in the urethra or outside the bladder needs urgent attention.

> **The aha** Measure the bladder. Then check the kidneys.

> **Pitfall** Ascites, a pelvic cyst, fluid-filled bowel and a full uterus mimic a bladder. Confirm the structure in two planes, find its position behind the pubic symphysis, and look for the bladder wall.

## After the catheter

Expect a brisk diuresis after relief of chronic retention with renal impairment, and monitor urine output and electrolytes. If symptoms persist or output is lower than expected, rescan the bladder and the catheter balloon.

?? Which planes do you need to estimate bladder volume?
+ Transverse for width and depth, sagittal for length
- Transverse only
- Sagittal only
- Coronal through the flank
: The ellipsoid formula needs three diameters. The transverse view gives width and depth, and the sagittal view gives the head-to-foot length.

?? What factor do you multiply the three bladder diameters by to estimate volume?
+ 0.52
- 1.0
- 0.25
- 3.14
: The bladder approximates an ellipsoid, and 0.52 (π/6) is the ellipsoid factor. The result is an estimate, so record it and compare with the volume drained.

?? A patient in retention has a large bladder and bilateral hydronephrosis. What does this suggest?
+ Obstruction severe or prolonged enough to affect the upper tracts
- Bilateral renal stones
- Normal findings in a full bladder
- Acute pyelonephritis
: Back pressure from a large retained bladder dilates both collecting systems. Check renal function and monitor for diuresis after catheterisation.

?? A catheterised patient has a full bladder on scan with little in the bag. What do you look for?
+ The catheter balloon position, and signs of a blocked catheter
- Free fluid in the right upper quadrant
- Ureteric jets
- Bladder wall thickness
: A full bladder around a catheter means it is blocked or not in the bladder. Find the balloon. A balloon in the urethra needs urgent attention.
