The retropharyngeal space lies between the back wall of the pharynx and the prevertebral muscles in front of the spine. In children under five it holds lymph nodes that drain the nose and throat; after a cold or tonsillitis they can suppurate and form an abscess. Adults get it after a penetrating injury such as a fish bone, after instrumentation, or with immunosuppression. It can threaten the airway, spread down into the mediastinum or involve the internal jugular vein.

## The scan

1. Scan only if the patient is calm and the airway is safe. A child with stridor or drooling stays upright with a parent, and the airway team comes first.
2. Use a high-frequency linear probe on the upper lateral neck, in front of the sternocleidomastoid, in transverse and long axis.
3. Identify the carotid artery and internal jugular vein with colour Doppler, and the pharynx medially.
4. Look medial and deep to the vessels, behind the pharynx and in front of the prevertebral muscles.
5. Scan both sides and compare.

## Reading the view

- **Normal:** a thin layer of soft tissue between the back of the pharynx and the prevertebral muscles.
- **Cellulitis or phlegmon:** thickened, dark, heterogeneous soft tissue with no discrete pocket.
- **Abscess:** a hypoechoic or complex collection with internal echoes behind the pharynx, often with surrounding oedema and no flow inside it.
- **Check the vein:** a compressible internal jugular vein with flow; an echogenic, non-compressible vein suggests thrombosis.

> **The aha** If the airway is threatened, treat the patient before the picture.

> **Pitfall** Ultrasound sees the deep neck spaces poorly. Evidence for this scan comes from small studies, mostly in children, so a normal or unclear scan never excludes an abscess. Never lie a child with stridor flat or upset them to get a view.

## Action

Senior airway help and ENT early. Contrast-enhanced CT of the neck defines the collection and its spread when it is safe to transfer. Give intravenous antibiotics. Ultrasound can help ENT plan or guide drainage once the collection is confirmed.

?? Who most commonly gets a retropharyngeal abscess?
+ A child under five after an upper respiratory infection
- A teenager after an ankle sprain
- An adult with gallstones
- A newborn after delivery
: Retropharyngeal lymph nodes are prominent in young children and regress with age.

?? A child is drooling, upright and has stridor. What do you do first?
+ Keep them calm and upright, and call senior airway help and ENT
- Lie them flat for a neck ultrasound
- Examine the throat with a tongue depressor
- Send them alone for a CT scan
: The airway comes first. The scan waits, or does not happen.

?? Where does the retropharyngeal space lie?
+ Between the back wall of the pharynx and the prevertebral muscles
- In front of the thyroid gland
- Inside the parotid gland
- Between the skin and the platysma
: From the lateral neck, look medial and deep to the carotid sheath, behind the pharynx.

?? On the scan, the tissue behind the pharynx is thick and heterogeneous, with no discrete pocket. What does it suggest?
+ Cellulitis or phlegmon, which may still progress, so CT and ENT decide
- A definite drainable abscess
- A normal finding
- A carotid aneurysm
: A drainable abscess is a discrete collection. Phlegmon can mature into one.

?? The scan of the retropharyngeal space looks normal in a feverish child with neck stiffness. What next?
+ Continue the work-up: a normal scan does not exclude an abscess
- Discharge: the scan rules it out
- Repeat the scan in a week
- Stop antibiotics
: Ultrasound sees the deep neck poorly. CT and ENT review follow if the picture fits.
