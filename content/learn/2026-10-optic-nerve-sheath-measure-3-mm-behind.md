The optic nerve sheath is continuous with the meninges, so a rise in intracranial pressure can distend it within minutes. The bedside scan takes a few minutes in a patient you are already worried about: headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness or a head injury. Studies report good accuracy, but they use different cut-offs, mostly between about 5 and 6 mm in adults, and the measurement depends heavily on technique.

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Do not scan if you suspect an open globe.
2. Select the ophthalmic or ocular preset, which keeps the power within the limits set for the eye.
3. Ask the patient to close the eye and look straight ahead. Cover the eyelid with a thick layer of gel and rest your hand on the face, so the probe floats with no pressure.
4. Find the dark band of the optic nerve running straight back from the globe, centred on the screen.
5. Measure 3 mm back from the retina along the nerve, then measure across the sheath at that depth, outer edge to outer edge.
6. Repeat in the other plane and in the other eye. Average the readings.

## Reading the view

- **Normal:** a sheath up to about 5 mm across in adults; smaller in children.
- **Wide:** above about 5 to 6 mm in adults. A clue to raised intracranial pressure.
- **Supporting signs:** a bulging optic disc into the globe (papilloedema) adds weight.

> **The aha** Measure 3 mm behind the globe, in two planes, in both eyes.

> **Pitfall** An oblique cut, a shadow from the back of the globe mistaken for the nerve, or probe pressure gives a false reading. The sheath can stay wide after the pressure falls, and a normal value does not exclude raised pressure.

## Action

Do not delay CT, neurosurgical advice or treatment to scan the eye. Use a wide sheath to escalate faster, and record both eyes and both planes so the next measurement can be compared.
