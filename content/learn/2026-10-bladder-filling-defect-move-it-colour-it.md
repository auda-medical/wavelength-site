A patient with visible haematuria or clot retention often has echogenic material in the bladder. Clot, debris and a bladder tumour all look bright. Two quick checks, movement and colour, separate most of them at the bedside.

![Wavelength Pearl: bladder filling defect. Reposition the patient to see if it moves, then use colour Doppler to look for internal flow.](/learn/images/bladder-clot-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Scan the bladder with the curvilinear probe just above the pubic symphysis, in transverse and then sagittal. The scan works best with some urine in the bladder.
2. Find the echogenic material and note where it sits.
3. Ask the patient to roll onto one side, or tilt the bed, and scan again. Watch whether the material moves or changes shape.
4. Add colour Doppler over the material with a low velocity scale.
5. Look at where it meets the bladder wall.

## Reading the view

- **Clot:** lies in the dependent part of the bladder, moves or changes shape when the patient moves, and has no internal flow. Flow at the edge is common, from the bladder wall beside it.
- **Attached wall lesion:** stays fixed to the wall when the patient moves and often shows vessels inside it on colour Doppler.
- **Catheter:** a bright double line with a round balloon. Check where it sits if the patient has one.

> **The aha** Move it. Colour it. Then decide the next step.

> **Pitfall** No flow does not prove a clot, and an organised clot moves little. A tumour also bleeds and forms clot around itself. Persistent or uncertain findings need urology assessment.

## What to do next

Visible haematuria with clot needs urology input, and clot retention needs a three-way catheter and bladder washout. Follow your local haematuria pathway, including the suspected cancer referral criteria for visible haematuria.

?? Echogenic material lies on the dependent wall of the bladder. What do you do first to tell clot from a wall lesion?
+ Reposition the patient and rescan to see if it moves
- Measure its diameter
- Press firmly with the probe
- Switch to the linear probe
: Clot is not attached, so it moves or changes shape when the patient moves. A lesion growing from the wall stays fixed.

?? Which colour Doppler finding favours a bladder wall lesion over clot?
+ Vessels inside the mass
- No flow inside the mass
- Flow in the bladder wall next to the mass
- Twinkling artefact behind a bright focus
: Clot has no blood supply, though flow appears in the wall beside it. A tumour often shows internal vessels.

?? An elderly patient has visible haematuria and an avascular echogenic mass that does not move. What is the safest conclusion?
+ It is indeterminate and needs urology assessment
- It is clot and needs no follow-up
- It is a bladder stone
- It is the catheter balloon
: Organised clot moves little and some tumours show little flow on bedside Doppler. A fixed mass with visible haematuria needs formal assessment.
