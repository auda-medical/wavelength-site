Distal biceps tears usually happen in middle-aged men lifting a heavy weight with the elbow bent. Repair works best in the first few weeks, before the tendon retracts and scars, so a missed tear costs the patient strength in supination. The examination can mislead. An intact bicipital aponeurosis, the lacertus fibrosus, holds the torn tendon close to the elbow crease, so the muscle bulge may not move far.

## The scan

1. Seat the patient with the elbow extended and the forearm supinated, resting on a pillow.
2. Use the high-frequency linear probe, long axis, over the front of the elbow.
3. Find the tendon above the elbow crease and follow it down.
4. The tendon dives deep towards the radius. Press the lower end of the probe harder than the upper end, so the beam stays at 90° to the fibres.
5. Follow it to the radial tuberosity, then confirm in short axis.
6. Rotate the forearm slowly from supination to pronation while you watch. The tuberosity turns, and an intact tendon moves with it.
7. Compare with the other arm.

## Reading the view

- **Normal.** A continuous, bright, fibrillar tendon inserting on the radial tuberosity.
- **Complete tear.** An empty tuberosity, a wavy retracted stump higher up, and dark fluid or haematoma in the tendon path.
- **Partial tear.** A thickened or thinned tendon with dark areas but some fibres still reaching the bone.

> **The aha** Trace to the insertion, not just to the elbow crease. The empty destination makes the diagnosis.

> **Pitfall** The distal tendon runs at an angle, so anisotropy makes a normal tendon look dark near the bone. Correct the angle before you call a tear. If the story fits and the scan is unclear, refer to the upper limb or orthopaedic team for review and MRI rather than reassure.

?? Why can a distal biceps tendon look intact in the elbow crease after a complete tear?
+ An intact lacertus fibrosus can hold the torn tendon close to its normal position
- The tendon always tears at the musculotendinous junction
- Anisotropy hides retraction
- The tendon reattaches within hours
: The bicipital aponeurosis links the biceps to the forearm fascia. When it stays intact, it limits retraction, so the tendon may sit near the elbow crease even though it has torn from the radial tuberosity.

?? Where must you follow the distal biceps tendon to exclude a complete tear?
+ To its insertion on the radial tuberosity
- To the lateral epicondyle
- To the olecranon
- To the coracoid process
: The distal biceps inserts on the radial tuberosity. Seeing continuous fibres reach the bone confirms the insertion. An empty tuberosity confirms a tear.

?? The distal tendon looks dark near the bone in long axis. What do you do first?
+ Press the lower end of the probe to bring the beam to 90° to the fibres
- Diagnose a partial tear
- Increase the depth
- Switch to colour Doppler
: The tendon dives deep towards the radius, so the beam often meets it at an angle. Anisotropy turns normal fibres dark. Correct the angle, then judge.

?? Which forearm movement helps you see the insertion?
+ Rotating from supination to pronation while you watch the tuberosity
- Flexing the wrist against resistance
- Abducting the shoulder
- Extending the fingers
: The biceps is a strong supinator. Rotating the forearm turns the tuberosity and moves an intact tendon with it. A torn tendon does not follow.

?? Why does a distal biceps rupture need early referral?
+ Surgical repair gives the best results before the tendon retracts and scars
- It always causes compartment syndrome
- It needs urgent vascular repair
- It heals fully with a sling alone
: Repair is easier and more successful in the first few weeks. Delay allows retraction and scarring, which can make repair more difficult.
