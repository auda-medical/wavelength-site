A diagnostic or therapeutic pleural aspiration is a common emergency procedure, and its complications come from the needle going somewhere other than the fluid: lung, liver, spleen or an intercostal artery. The British Thoracic Society advises ultrasound guidance for pleural procedures for fluid, because it reduces pneumothorax and organ puncture. A chest X-ray shows that fluid exists. Only the scan shows where it is safe to put a needle.

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Sit the patient upright, leaning forward over a table, in the position you will use for the procedure. If they cannot sit, use the lateral position with the effusion side down and scan from the back.
2. Use the curvilinear probe, or the phased array between narrow ribs, on the posterolateral chest.
3. Start low at the flank, find the liver or spleen and the bright diaphragm, then sweep upwards until fluid appears above it.
4. Watch through several breaths. The lung drifts down into the pocket on inspiration, and the diaphragm, with the liver or spleen, rises on expiration.
5. Choose the intercostal space with the deepest pocket that stays clear of lung and diaphragm throughout the cycle, usually at least one space above the diaphragm's highest point.
6. Measure skin to fluid (how far the needle must go) and the depth of fluid at that point (your margin). Many operators look for at least 1 cm of fluid throughout breathing; more is safer.
7. Check the space with colour Doppler for the intercostal artery, and mark the site. Aspirate straight away, without moving the patient, or under real-time guidance.

## Reading the view

- **Good window:** a deep black pocket, the diaphragm well below it on every breath, and lung well away from the needle path.
- **Unsafe window:** a shallow pocket, lung sliding into the path on inspiration, or the diaphragm, liver or spleen rising into the path on expiration.
- **Complex fluid:** septations, debris or swirl change the plan. See [Pleural effusion: simple or septated?](/learn/pleural-effusion-septated-ultrasound/).

> **The aha** See the diaphragm. See the fluid. Then choose the site.

> **Pitfall** A blunted angle on the X-ray is not a needle site, and a mark made minutes earlier in another position is not a safe one. Close to the spine the intercostal artery is tortuous and unprotected, so stay lateral and enter just above the upper edge of the rib below.

## Action

Follow your local pleural procedure and consent process. For a therapeutic aspiration, stop at about 1.5 litres, or earlier if the patient develops chest tightness or coughing, to reduce the risk of re-expansion pulmonary oedema. Send fluid for protein, LDH, pH (on a blood gas analyser, unless frankly purulent), glucose, microscopy, culture and cytology as the picture suggests.

?? Why use ultrasound rather than the chest X-ray to choose the site?
+ It shows the diaphragm, the organs and the lung moving relative to the fluid, which an X-ray cannot
- It shows the fluid volume more accurately than any other test
- It makes a coagulation check unnecessary
- The X-ray is always normal in effusions
: Ultrasound guidance reduces pneumothorax and organ puncture. The X-ray confirms fluid; the scan finds a safe site.

?? On inspiration, lung slides down into your chosen pocket. What do you do?
+ Choose another space or level where the pocket stays clear through the whole cycle
- Ask the patient to hold their breath for the whole procedure
- Use a longer needle
- Proceed, because the lung moves away on expiration
: The pocket must stay clear of lung and of diaphragm through every phase of breathing.

?? Where should the needle cross the intercostal space?
+ Just above the upper edge of the rib below
- Just below the lower edge of the rib above
- Exactly midway, close to the spine
- It does not matter
: The intercostal vessels and nerve run along the lower edge of each rib. Near the spine the artery is tortuous and less protected, so stay lateral.

?? You marked the site with the patient sitting. They then lie back to rest. What next?
+ Rescan and remark in the procedure position before inserting the needle
- Use the mark: the fluid will not have moved
- Insert the needle 1 cm lower than the mark
- Insert the needle 1 cm higher than the mark
: Fluid, lung and diaphragm shift with position. Mark and aspirate in the same position, without delay.

?? During a therapeutic aspiration the patient starts coughing and feels chest tightness. What do you do?
+ Stop the aspiration
- Speed up the drainage to finish sooner
- Remove at least 2 litres before stopping
- Lie the patient flat and continue
: Chest tightness or coughing can herald re-expansion pulmonary oedema. Stop, and limit a single aspiration to about 1.5 litres.
