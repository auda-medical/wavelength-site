The IVC is easy to find and easy to measure, so it often gets the final word on fluids. Its size and collapse reflect right atrial pressure, which breathing, ventilation, the right heart and the abdomen all change. Use it as one clue among several.

![Wavelength Pearl: IVC. Measure size and collapse, then put the IVC in context with the heart and lungs before deciding on fluid.](/learn/images/ivc-context-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Find the IVC in long axis from the subcostal window with the curvilinear or phased-array probe, and follow it into the right atrium so you know it is not the aorta.
2. Measure about 2 cm from the right atrium, just beyond the hepatic vein, at end expiration.
3. Watch it with quiet breathing, then with a sniff, and estimate the collapse.
4. Scan the heart: right ventricle size, left ventricle function and the pericardium.
5. Scan the lungs for B-lines.

## Reading the view

- **Small and collapsing:** 2.1 cm or less, collapsing more than 50% with a sniff, suggests a low right atrial pressure. In a hypotensive patient with a vigorous heart and clear lungs, this supports a cautious fluid trial.
- **Large and fixed:** over 2.1 cm, collapsing less than 50%, suggests a raised right atrial pressure. Fluid overload, right heart strain, tamponade, tension pneumothorax and raised abdominal pressure all cause it.
- **In between:** the IVC adds little. Rely on the rest of the assessment.

> **The aha** The IVC adds context. It does not write the fluid prescription.

> **Pitfall** Mechanical ventilation, strong breathing effort, right heart disease and raised abdominal pressure all distort IVC size and collapse. A plethoric IVC in a ventilated patient means little on its own.

## Putting it together

A plethoric IVC with a dilated right ventricle points to right heart strain. A plethoric IVC with bilateral B-lines points to fluid overload. A small, collapsing IVC with a hyperdynamic left ventricle and clear lungs fits hypovolaemia or vasodilatation. Reassess after every fluid bolus.

?? Where do you measure the IVC diameter?
+ About 2 cm from the right atrium, at end expiration
- At the level of the renal veins
- Where it crosses the aorta
- At its narrowest point during inspiration
: Guidelines measure just beyond the hepatic vein junction, about 2 cm from the right atrium, at end expiration, in long axis.

?? An IVC of 2.5 cm collapses less than 50% with a sniff. What does this suggest?
+ Raised right atrial pressure
- Hypovolaemia
- A fluid-responsive patient
- Normal right atrial pressure
: A wide IVC with little collapse suggests a high right atrial pressure. It does not tell you the cause, so look at the right ventricle, the pericardium and the lungs.

?? A ventilated patient has a plethoric IVC. What do you conclude?
+ Positive pressure ventilation distorts the IVC, so interpret it with other findings
- The patient is fluid overloaded
- The patient has a pulmonary embolism
- The patient needs a diuretic
: Positive pressure raises intrathoracic pressure and distends the IVC. In ventilated patients, a dilated IVC alone does not guide fluid decisions.

?? A hypotensive patient has a plethoric IVC and bilateral B-lines. What is the best reading?
+ Fluid overload or pulmonary oedema is likely, so look for another cause before giving fluid
- Hypovolaemia, so give a fluid bolus
- Pneumothorax
- Normal findings
: A full IVC with widespread B-lines suggests raised filling pressures and lung water. More fluid risks harm. Look at the heart for poor function or tamponade.
