Palmar lacerations, foreign bodies and burns of the hand often need more than skin infiltration. A median nerve block in the forearm numbs the radial side of the palm and the palmar surface of the thumb, index, middle and half of the ring finger. It needs only a few millilitres, avoids distorting the wound and, combined with the ulnar and radial blocks, numbs the whole hand. A small emergency department study showed ultrasound-guided forearm blocks were feasible for hand procedures.

## Before you block

1. Record motor and sensory function in all three nerve territories, including two-point discrimination, before the block. A tendon or nerve injury must be documented first.
2. Record consent and calculate the dose. Three to five millilitres per nerve is usually enough.

## The scan

1. Rest the forearm palm up on a support.
2. Place the linear probe transversely on the front of the forearm, a few centimetres above the wrist crease.
3. Identify the flexor tendons and look for the median nerve, an oval with a honeycomb texture, lying between flexor digitorum superficialis and profundus. Close to the wrist it moves superficial to the tendons.
4. Slide up and down. The tendons change into muscle as you move up the forearm. The nerve keeps its honeycomb look all the way.
5. Insert the needle in plane from either side, staying beside the nerve, not in it.
6. Inject small amounts slowly, moving the tip above and below the nerve, until a ring of fluid surrounds it.

## Reading the view

- **Median nerve.** A honeycomb oval which keeps its shape as you slide up and down.
- **Tendons.** Bright, fibrillar ovals which turn into muscle higher in the forearm.
- **Correct spread.** A dark ring, or halo, of fluid around the nerve.
- **Wrong spread.** The nerve itself swelling, or fluid collecting away from it.

> **The aha** Slide the probe up the forearm. Tendons turn into muscle, the nerve does not. That is how you tell them apart.

> **Pitfall** The palmar cutaneous branch leaves the median nerve above the wrist. Block in the mid to distal forearm, not at the wrist crease, to cover the skin of the thenar palm. Stop at once if the patient reports electric pain or you feel high resistance.

?? Where does the median nerve lie in the mid forearm?
+ Between flexor digitorum superficialis and flexor digitorum profundus
- Beside the radial artery under brachioradialis
- On the back of the forearm with the extensor tendons
- Beside the ulnar artery under flexor carpi ulnaris
: In the mid forearm the median nerve runs between the two finger flexor muscles. Near the wrist it moves superficial to the tendons.

?? How do you tell the median nerve from a tendon?
+ Slide up the forearm: tendons become muscle while the nerve keeps its honeycomb look
- The nerve is always black
- Tendons pulsate
- The nerve is always deeper than the bone
: Tracking the structure up and down the forearm separates the two. Tendons join their muscle bellies. The nerve continues unchanged.

?? Why do you block above the wrist rather than at the wrist crease?
+ The palmar cutaneous branch leaves above the wrist, so a wrist block misses the skin of the thenar palm
- The nerve is too deep at the wrist
- The radial artery crosses the nerve at the wrist
- The block works only in the upper arm
: The palmar cutaneous branch arises several centimetres above the wrist and supplies the skin over the thenar eminence.

?? Which area does a median nerve block numb?
+ The radial side of the palm and the palmar surfaces of the thumb, index and middle fingers
- The little finger
- The back of the thumb and first web space
- The whole forearm
: The median nerve supplies the radial palm and the palmar surface of the thumb, index, middle and half of the ring finger.

?? The patient reports sharp electric pain during injection. What do you do?
+ Stop, withdraw slightly and reposition
- Inject faster
- Continue, as this confirms the right spot
- Add a larger volume
: Electric pain or high resistance suggests the tip is in the nerve. Stop and reposition to avoid nerve injury.
