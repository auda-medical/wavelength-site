A swollen, painful calf sends you looking for a clot. When the veins compress, the answer often sits behind the knee. A Baker's cyst, or popliteal cyst, is a fluid-filled bursa connected to the knee joint. When it ruptures, fluid tracks down the calf and the leg looks exactly like a DVT. The two can also occur together.

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Complete the proximal compression scan of the femoral and popliteal veins first, or as part of the same scan.
2. With the knee slightly flexed, place the linear probe transversely over the medial side of the popliteal fossa.
3. Find the medial head of gastrocnemius and the semimembranosus tendon beside it.
4. Look for a collection between them, with a narrow neck running towards the joint.
5. Scan the cyst in two planes and check its lower end.
6. Follow the medial calf downwards, between gastrocnemius and soleus, looking for tracking fluid.
7. Switch on colour Doppler over any round collection behind the knee.

## Reading the view

- **Intact cyst.** A smooth, dark collection with a neck between semimembranosus and the medial head of gastrocnemius. It may contain debris or septations.
- **Ruptured cyst.** The lower end becomes pointed or irregular, and a thin layer of fluid tracks down between the calf muscles or under the skin.
- **Popliteal artery aneurysm.** A round structure behind the knee with flow inside on colour Doppler. It is not a cyst.

> **The aha** Find the neck. A collection with a neck between semimembranosus and the medial head of gastrocnemius is a Baker's cyst.

> **Pitfall** Finding a cyst does not exclude a DVT. A large cyst can compress the popliteal vein, and both can exist together. Always complete the compression scan.

?? Where does a Baker's cyst arise?
+ Between the semimembranosus tendon and the medial head of gastrocnemius
- Between the biceps femoris and the lateral head of gastrocnemius
- Within the popliteal vein
- In front of the patella
: A Baker's cyst is a distended gastrocnemius-semimembranosus bursa. It connects to the knee joint through a neck between these two structures, on the medial side of the popliteal fossa.

?? Which finding suggests a Baker's cyst has ruptured?
+ A pointed lower end with fluid tracking down between the calf muscles
- A smooth, round collection with a neck to the joint
- Flow inside the collection on colour Doppler
- A compressible popliteal vein
: When the cyst leaks, fluid dissects down the calf, often between gastrocnemius and soleus. The cyst loses its smooth, rounded lower end.

?? You find a ruptured Baker's cyst in a patient with a swollen calf. What do you do next?
+ Complete the compression scan of the femoral and popliteal veins
- Stop the scan, as the cyst explains the swelling
- Aspirate the cyst in the emergency department
- Start anticoagulation without further scanning
: A Baker's cyst and a DVT can occur together, and a large cyst can compress the popliteal vein. The cyst does not replace the compression scan.

?? Why switch on colour Doppler over a round collection behind the knee?
+ To exclude a popliteal artery aneurysm
- To measure the cyst volume
- To see the neck of the cyst
- To check the knee joint for effusion
: A popliteal artery aneurysm can look like a cyst on greyscale. Flow inside the structure on colour Doppler changes the diagnosis and the referral.

?? A 65-year-old with knee osteoarthritis has a painful, swollen calf and bruising around the ankle. The veins compress normally. What is the most likely diagnosis?
+ A ruptured Baker's cyst
- Proximal DVT
- Achilles tendon rupture
- Cellulitis
: Fluid tracking down from a ruptured cyst can cause calf swelling and bruising around the ankle, the crescent sign. Knee osteoarthritis is a common cause of a Baker's cyst. Normal compression makes proximal DVT unlikely.
