Bedside ultrasound in suspected renal colic looks for hydronephrosis, and rules out alternatives such as an abdominal aortic aneurysm. The bladder adds one more clue. Every few seconds, peristalsis squirts urine from each ureter into the bladder. Colour Doppler shows this as a jet. A complete obstruction stops it.

![Wavelength Pearl: renal colic. Colour Doppler over a full bladder shows ureteric jets. A silent jet on the painful side supports obstruction.](/learn/images/ureteric-jets-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Scan with a reasonably full bladder. Offer a drink if the patient can have one.
2. Use the curvilinear probe in transverse above the pubic symphysis, angled down towards the base of the bladder, the trigone.
3. Find the two ureteric orifices at the back of the bladder base.
4. Switch on colour Doppler with the scale set low, and the box over both orifices.
5. Hold still and watch for at least two to three minutes. Jets come intermittently.
6. Then scan both kidneys for hydronephrosis.

## Reading the view

- **Normal.** A flame of colour from each orifice, crossing the bladder towards the middle.
- **Absent jet on the painful side**, with jets on the other side, supports obstruction on that side.
- **Weak jets on both sides** suggest a low urine output or an underfilled bladder, not obstruction. Rehydrate and rescan.

> **The aha** A silent jet is more convincing with hydronephrosis on the same side. Look at both before you decide.

> **Pitfall** Jets are intermittent. A short look or an empty bladder gives a false negative. A jet on the painful side does not exclude a partial obstruction.

## Where it fits

In the STONE trial, starting with point-of-care ultrasound rather than CT in suspected renal colic reduced radiation exposure, with no increase in high-risk missed diagnoses. Ultrasound does not replace CT for stone size and position, or when the diagnosis is uncertain.

- A patient over 50 with first-time renal colic needs their aorta scanned.
- Fever with an obstructed kidney is an emergency. It needs urgent urology review for drainage.

?? What does a ureteric jet show on colour Doppler?
+ Urine entering the bladder from the ureter
- Blood flow in the bladder wall
- Reflux of urine into the ureter
- A stone moving in the ureter
: Ureteric peristalsis squirts urine into the bladder every few seconds. Colour Doppler detects the moving urine as a flame of colour from the ureteric orifice.

?? A patient with left loin pain has a right ureteric jet and no left jet after three minutes. The left kidney shows moderate hydronephrosis. What does this support?
+ Obstruction of the left ureter
- A normal finding
- Right ureteric obstruction
- Bladder outlet obstruction
: An absent jet on the painful side, with hydronephrosis on the same side, supports ureteric obstruction. Bladder outlet obstruction affects both kidneys and both jets.

?? Both ureteric jets look weak, and the bladder is almost empty. What is the most likely explanation?
+ Low urine output or poor bladder filling
- Bilateral ureteric stones
- A normal finding in renal colic
- Bladder rupture
: Jets depend on urine production. A dry patient or an empty bladder gives weak or absent jets on both sides. Rehydrate and rescan before reading anything into it.

?? Why do you watch for at least two to three minutes?
+ Jets come intermittently, so a short look misses them
- The bladder needs time to fill
- Colour Doppler takes time to warm up
- The patient needs time to stop breathing
: Ureteric peristalsis produces jets every few seconds to a minute or more. A short look gives a false impression of an absent jet.

?? A 68-year-old presents with first-time left loin pain and haematuria. What else must you scan?
+ The abdominal aorta
- The gallbladder
- The appendix
- The femoral veins
: A leaking abdominal aortic aneurysm presents with loin pain and haematuria and mimics renal colic. In a patient over 50 with first-time renal colic, scan the aorta.
