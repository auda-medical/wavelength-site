Septic arthritis of the sternoclavicular joint is uncommon, which is why it is missed. Injecting drug use, diabetes, immunosuppression, a central venous line and infection elsewhere are the usual risk factors. The joint is small and superficial, so ultrasound can show an effusion within a minute, and the other side is right next to it for comparison. Like the [hip](/learn/hip-effusion-ultrasound/) and the [wrist](/learn/wrist-effusion-ultrasound/), the sign is fluid lifting the capsule away from the bone.

![Wavelength Pearl: sternoclavicular joint. The normal joint with a thin capsule between the clavicle and manubrium, and an effusion lifting the capsule.](/learn/images/sternoclavicular-effusion-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Lie the patient flat with the head straight.
2. Use a high-frequency linear probe across the joint: along the medial clavicle, onto the manubrium.
3. Find the two bright bone surfaces and the narrow gap between them.
4. Turn the probe 90 degrees for a second plane.
5. Scan the other joint with the same settings and compare.

## Reading the view

- **Normal:** two bright cortices, the clavicle head and the manubrium, each with a shadow beneath, a narrow dark gap and a thin capsule lying close to the bone.
- **Effusion:** an anechoic or complex pocket that lifts the capsule into a convex bulge over the joint.
- **Beyond the joint:** fluid tracking into the soft tissue of the chest wall, or an irregular, broken cortex, suggests an abscess or osteomyelitis.

> **The aha** Compare both sides: a small bulge is easier to see next to its twin.

> **Pitfall** Fluid does not prove infection: osteoarthritis, trauma and inflammatory arthritis cause effusions too. Ultrasound cannot see behind the joint into the mediastinum. The great vessels and pleura lie close behind, so do not aspirate blind.

## Action

With fever or raised inflammatory markers, take blood cultures and treat as possible septic arthritis. CT or MRI shows deep extension. Aspiration, if needed, is image-guided by an experienced operator, with orthopaedic review.
