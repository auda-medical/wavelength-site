Acute mesenteric ischaemia is uncommon, lethal and easily missed. An embolus to the superior mesenteric artery, usually from atrial fibrillation, is the classic cause; thrombosis on an atheromatous artery, low-flow states and mesenteric venous thrombosis also cause it. The early abdomen can be soft while the pain is severe. Mortality climbs the longer it goes unrecognised, so the diagnosis has to be considered before the bloods or the scan turn abnormal.

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Use the curvilinear probe to sweep the whole abdomen, starting at the tender area, and look for free fluid.
2. Switch to the linear probe to see the layers of the bowel wall in a thinner patient.
3. Measure the wall of any abnormal loop.
4. Add colour Doppler with a low velocity scale, and compare the wall flow with a normal loop.
5. Look in the liver for gas in the portal veins.

## Reading the view

- **Normal bowel:** a thin, layered wall, usually under 3 mm, with some flow in it and visible peristalsis.
- **Worrying loop:** a thick wall, little or no flow, reduced peristalsis and free fluid beside it.
- **Late signs:** bright gas in the bowel wall with dirty shadowing (pneumatosis), or [gas in the portal veins](/learn/portal-venous-gas-pneumobilia-ultrasound/).
- **Also consider:** [obstruction with strangulation](/learn/small-bowel-obstruction-ultrasound/), which can look similar.

> **The aha** Pain out of proportion: think of the gut, then image it.

> **Pitfall** Early ischaemia can look normal, because the wall thickens late, and gas in the abdomen often hides the bowel. A normal lactate does not exclude it either. A normal scan must never stop the request for CT angiography.

## Action

Resuscitate, give analgesia and broad-spectrum antibiotics, and call the surgeons and vascular team early. Request urgent CT angiography, and do not wait for the creatinine if the picture fits. Anticoagulation and the route to revascularisation are decided with the specialist teams.

?? Which patient should make you think of acute mesenteric ischaemia?
+ An older patient with atrial fibrillation, severe abdominal pain and a soft abdomen
- A young patient with a week of mild, resolving diarrhoea
- A patient with pain only on moving the shoulder
- A child with a painless groin lump
: Pain out of proportion to the examination, with a source of embolus, is the classic pattern.

?? How does a normal small bowel wall look?
+ Thin and layered, with flow in the wall and visible peristalsis
- Over 1 cm thick and uniformly dark
- Bright, with a dirty shadow in the wall
- Invisible on every scan
: Compare any abnormal loop with a normal one nearby.

?? Which combination raises concern for ischaemia?
+ A thick bowel wall with little or no flow, and free fluid
- A thin wall with normal flow and peristalsis
- A full bladder with a smooth wall
- A normal gallbladder
: Together with the clinical picture, these signs need urgent imaging and surgical review.

?? What does bright gas inside the bowel wall suggest?
+ Pneumatosis, a late and serious sign
- A normal finding after a meal
- A kidney stone
- Early, reversible ischaemia only
: Gas in the wall or the portal veins suggests advanced injury, though these signs have other causes too.

?? The scan and the lactate are normal, but the pain is severe and the patient has atrial fibrillation. What next?
+ Urgent CT angiography and surgical review
- Discharge, because both tests were normal
- Repeat the scan next week
- Wait for the lactate to rise
: Early ischaemia can be normal on both tests. Delay is what kills.
