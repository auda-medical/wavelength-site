B-lines are bright vertical lines which start at the pleural line, reach the bottom of the screen without fading, and move with lung sliding. They tell you the lung under the probe has lost some of its air. They do not tell you why. The pattern and the pleural line do.

![Wavelength Pearl: B-lines. Focal B-lines beside an irregular pleura, diffuse B-lines with a smooth pleura, and aerated lung with A-lines.](/learn/images/b-lines-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are fabricated educational illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Scan both sides of the chest in the same zones, not one striking interspace.
2. Keep depth, focus and gain sensible. Settings change how many B-lines you see.
3. In each zone, record whether B-lines are absent, focal, patchy or diffuse.
4. Look at the pleural line: smooth or irregular, continuous or broken.
5. Look for small consolidations under the pleura, spared areas, effusion and moving air bronchograms.

## Reading the pattern

- **Diffuse B-lines on both sides with a smooth pleural line** support cardiogenic pulmonary oedema in the right setting.
- **Focal B-lines** next to an irregular pleura or a small consolidation suggest a local cause such as pneumonia, contusion or infarction.
- **Patchy B-lines on both sides, with spared areas and an irregular pleura,** fit inflammatory lung injury, though the patterns overlap.

> **The aha** Map the pattern before you name the disease: focal, patchy or diffuse, smooth or irregular pleura.

> **Pitfall** The number of B-lines changes with the probe, the frequency, focus, gain and the machine's processing. Counting without the same settings each time gives false precision.

## Action

Put the lung pattern together with the history, the examination, a focused heart scan and any earlier imaging. If you are following treatment, repeat the same zones with the same settings.

?? What do B-lines prove on their own?
+ Loss of air in the lung under the probe, not a single diagnosis
- Cardiogenic pulmonary oedema
- Bacterial pneumonia
- Pulmonary embolism
: B-lines appear in many interstitial and alveolar processes.

?? Which pattern best supports cardiogenic pulmonary oedema?
+ Diffuse B-lines on both sides with a smooth pleural line
- One cluster beside a small consolidation
- A single B-line at one lung base
- Absent sliding with A-lines
: The distribution and the pleural line help, though the clinical picture still decides.

?? What does a cluster of B-lines beside an irregular pleural line suggest?
+ A local process in the lung
- Normal lungs
- Fluid overload on its own
- Aortic dissection
: Pneumonia, contusion and infarction can all change the lung next to an abnormal pleural surface.

?? Why keep the settings the same on repeat scans?
+ Settings change how many B-lines you see
- B-lines come only from the colour map
- Depth never affects artefacts
- Repeat scans need a different probe each time
: The same settings make a change over time believable.

?? A breathless patient has B-lines on both sides. What is the best next step?
+ Read them with the clinical picture and a focused heart scan
- Diagnose heart failure without further assessment
- Ignore them, as artefacts are never useful
- Count every line for ten minutes before acting
: The pattern is useful when it is combined with the rest of the assessment.
