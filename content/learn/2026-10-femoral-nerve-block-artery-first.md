The femoral nerve supplies the front of the thigh, most of the femur, the front of the knee and, through the saphenous nerve, the inner side of the lower leg. A femoral nerve block gives fast analgesia for a femoral shaft fracture, a fractured neck of femur or a painful knee injury, and makes splinting and transfers bearable. In children with a femoral shaft fracture it is often the best first analgesic.

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## Before you block

1. Record the neurovascular examination of the leg, including the distal pulses.
2. Record consent, calculate the maximum safe dose of local anaesthetic, attach monitoring and know where the lipid emulsion is.
3. Think about compartment syndrome. In a high-risk injury, such as a tibial fracture, agree the plan for monitoring with the orthopaedic team first.

## The scan

1. Lie the patient supine with the leg straight.
2. Place the linear probe transversely just below the inguinal ligament, over the femoral pulse.
3. Find the femoral artery first: a round, black, pulsating circle. The femoral vein lies medial to it and collapses with pressure.
4. Look lateral to the artery for the nerve: a bright, flattened oval or triangle, lying on iliopsoas and under the bright line of fascia iliaca.
5. Insert the needle in plane from the lateral side, so the needle meets the nerve before it reaches the artery.
6. Pass through fascia iliaca, inject 1 to 2 mL, and watch the fluid lift the fascia and spread around the nerve. Then inject the rest, usually 10 to 20 mL.

## Reading the view

- **Femoral artery.** Round, black and pulsating. Colour Doppler confirms it.
- **Femoral nerve.** Bright and honeycomb in texture, lateral to the artery, deep to fascia iliaca.
- **Correct spread.** Dark fluid under fascia iliaca, wrapping above and below the nerve.
- **Wrong layer.** Fluid above fascia iliaca spreads in the fat and does not reach the nerve.

> **The aha** The nerve sits under fascia iliaca, not beside the artery in the same sheath. Feel a give as the needle passes the fascia, and see the fluid lift it.

> **Pitfall** The block weakens the quadriceps. Warn the patient and the ward team, and make sure no one tries to walk on the leg until the block has worn off.

?? Where does the femoral nerve lie in relation to the femoral artery at the inguinal crease?
+ Lateral to the artery, deep to fascia iliaca
- Medial to the femoral vein
- Inside the femoral sheath with the artery
- Superficial to fascia lata, over the artery
: From lateral to medial, the order is nerve, artery, vein. The nerve lies under fascia iliaca, outside the femoral sheath which holds the artery and vein.

?? Why do you insert the needle from the lateral side?
+ The needle reaches the nerve before it can reach the artery
- The nerve is easier to see from the medial side
- The lateral approach avoids fascia iliaca
- It gives a faster onset
: With a lateral to medial path, the needle meets the nerve first and keeps away from the femoral artery and vein.

?? Which area does a femoral nerve block not cover reliably?
+ The skin on the outer side of the thigh
- The front of the thigh
- The femoral shaft
- The front of the knee
: The outer thigh belongs to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve. A fascia iliaca block reaches it more reliably.

?? What does correct spread look like?
+ Dark fluid under fascia iliaca, surrounding the nerve
- Fluid in the fat above fascia iliaca
- A swelling inside the nerve
- Fluid around the femoral vein
: Fluid deep to fascia iliaca surrounds the nerve. Fluid above the fascia stays away from it, and swelling inside the nerve means the tip is in the nerve.

?? What do you tell the patient and ward team after the block?
+ The thigh muscles will be weak, so the patient must not walk on the leg until the block wears off
- The patient can walk straight away
- The leg will be warm and red for a week
- The block will last for 72 hours
: The femoral nerve supplies the quadriceps. Weakness can cause a fall until the block wears off.
