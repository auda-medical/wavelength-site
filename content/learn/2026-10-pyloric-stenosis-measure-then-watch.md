Pyloric stenosis typically presents between about 2 and 12 weeks of age with forceful, non-bilious vomiting after feeds and a hungry baby. The thickened muscle can sometimes be felt as an olive, but ultrasound is now the usual test. In emergency department studies, clinicians trained in point-of-care ultrasound have identified it accurately, though the studies are small. A formal scan or the paediatric surgeons confirm it.

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Bilious (green) vomiting is not pyloric stenosis until proven otherwise: think of malrotation and call the surgeons now.
2. Lay the baby supine, or turned slightly onto the right side so fluid fills the antrum. A small feed of milk or clear fluid helps.
3. Use a high-frequency linear probe below the liver, to the right of the midline. Find the gallbladder, then the stomach, and follow the antrum to the pylorus.
4. In short axis, look for the target: a thick dark ring of muscle around a bright centre.
5. In long axis, measure the single muscle wall thickness and the length of the channel.
6. Watch for several minutes. Does fluid pass through the pylorus?

## Reading the view

- **Normal:** a thin muscle wall, a short channel, and fluid passing into the duodenum as the pylorus opens.
- **Pyloric stenosis:** a single muscle wall of 3 mm or more and a channel of about 15 mm or more, which stays closed while the stomach churns against it.
- **Pylorospasm:** the muscle looks thick for a while, then relaxes and lets fluid through. Measurements are often borderline.

> **The aha** Measure it, then watch it open. Passage matters as well as numbers.

> **Pitfall** An oblique cut makes the muscle look thicker than it is, and an overfilled stomach pushes the pylorus out of view. Measure in a true long axis, and repeat after a few minutes if the result is borderline.

## Action

Refer to the paediatric surgeons. Check a blood gas and electrolytes: prolonged vomiting causes a low chloride, low potassium metabolic alkalosis, which must be corrected before surgery. Babies who are dehydrated, lethargic or bilious need urgent senior review.

?? What is the typical age for pyloric stenosis?
+ About 2 to 12 weeks
- The first 24 hours of life
- Around 1 year
- Around 5 years
: It typically presents in the first weeks of life, most often between 3 and 6 weeks.

?? Which measurements support pyloric stenosis?
+ Muscle wall 3 mm or more and channel about 15 mm or more
- Muscle wall 1 mm and channel 5 mm
- Any visible pylorus
- Muscle wall 10 mm and channel 50 mm only
: A thick wall and a long channel, together with failure to open, support the diagnosis.

?? The pylorus looks thick, then relaxes after a few minutes and fluid passes. What is the likely explanation?
+ Pylorospasm
- Definite pyloric stenosis
- Malrotation
- Intussusception
: Pylorospasm relaxes. Stenosis stays tight.

?? A 4-week-old has green vomiting. What should you do?
+ Treat it as possible malrotation and call the surgeons urgently
- Scan for pyloric stenosis and wait
- Give a feed and observe
- Discharge with reflux advice
: Bilious vomiting suggests obstruction beyond the pylorus. Malrotation with volvulus is an emergency.

?? Which blood picture is typical after prolonged vomiting from pyloric stenosis?
+ Low chloride, low potassium metabolic alkalosis
- High potassium metabolic acidosis
- Normal electrolytes always
- High chloride metabolic acidosis
: Vomiting loses acid and chloride. It is corrected before surgery.
