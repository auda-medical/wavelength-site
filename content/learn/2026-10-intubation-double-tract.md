Unrecognised oesophageal intubation kills. Waveform capnography is the standard for confirming tube position, and UK and international airway guidance is clear: no trace means wrong place. Capnography has limits in cardiac arrest, when low flow gives a flat trace even with the tube in the trachea. Ultrasound of the neck adds a quick, direct look.

![Wavelength Pearl: intubation check. One air-mucosa interface means tracheal intubation. A second interface beside the trachea, the double tract sign, means oesophageal intubation.](/learn/images/intubation-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Use the linear probe, transverse, just above the suprasternal notch.
2. Before intubation, find the trachea, a bright curved line with reverberation behind it, in the midline.
3. Find the oesophagus, usually behind and to the left of the trachea: a round structure with layered walls.
4. Hold the probe still during intubation and watch as the tube passes.
5. Confirm with waveform capnography.

## Reading the view

- **Tracheal intubation.** One air-mucosa interface in the midline. The tube passing makes a brief flutter in the trachea. The oesophagus stays collapsed.
- **Oesophageal intubation.** A second bright curved line with reverberation appears beside the trachea, where the oesophagus was. Two air shadows sit side by side. This is the double tract sign.

> **The aha** One tract good. Two tracts stop.

> **Pitfall** Scanning off the midline, or heavy probe pressure, distorts the view. Ultrasound confirms the tube is in the trachea, not how deep it sits. Check for bilateral lung sliding to exclude a main bronchus intubation, and always pair the scan with capnography.

## After the tube is in

Scan both sides of the chest. Lung sliding on both sides shows both lungs are ventilated. Sliding on the right only, with a lung pulse on the left, suggests the tube sits in the right main bronchus.

?? What is the double tract sign?
+ A second air-mucosa interface beside the trachea, showing oesophageal intubation
- The normal trachea and collapsed oesophagus before intubation
- Two lung pleural lines sliding together
- The tube cuff seen inside the trachea
: When the tube enters the oesophagus, air inside it creates a second bright curved line with reverberation, beside the trachea. Two air shadows mean the tube is in the wrong place.

?? Where does the oesophagus usually lie in a transverse view above the suprasternal notch?
+ Behind and to the left of the trachea
- In front of the trachea
- To the right of the carotid artery
- Inside the thyroid gland
: The oesophagus usually sits posterolateral to the trachea on the left. Find it before intubation so you know where to look for a second tract.

?? Which test remains the standard for confirming tracheal tube placement?
+ Waveform capnography
- Neck ultrasound alone
- Auscultation of the chest
- Misting in the tube
: Sustained waveform capnography is the standard. Ultrasound adds a direct look, especially when capnography is limited during cardiac arrest. Auscultation and misting are unreliable.

?? What does neck ultrasound not tell you about the tube?
+ How deep it sits, including main bronchus intubation
- Whether it is in the trachea
- Whether it is in the oesophagus
- Whether it passed during intubation
: The neck view shows whether the tube is in the trachea or the oesophagus. It does not show the tip position. Check lung sliding on both sides to detect a main bronchus intubation.

?? After intubation, the right chest shows sliding and the left shows a lung pulse with no sliding. What is the most likely cause?
+ Right main bronchus intubation
- Oesophageal intubation
- Left pneumothorax
- Normal ventilation
: A lung pulse shows the left lung lies against the chest wall but is not ventilating. With sliding on the right, the tube most likely sits in the right main bronchus. Withdraw it slightly and rescan.
