The supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block was described in 2011 as a way to place the injection higher, inside the pelvis, under the same fascia. Cadaver and clinical studies show more consistent spread to the femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves than the infra-inguinal approach, and it reduces opioid use after hip surgery. In the emergency department it suits a fractured neck of femur or a painful pubic ramus fracture, under local governance.

## Before you block

1. Record the neurovascular examination and consent, following your trust's process for a patient who lacks capacity.
2. Calculate the maximum safe dose. This block uses 30 to 40 mL.
3. Attach monitoring and know where the lipid emulsion is.

## The scan

1. Lie the patient supine.
2. Place the linear probe in a sagittal plane, medial to the anterior superior iliac spine, just above the inguinal ligament.
3. Identify iliacus lying in the iliac fossa, the bright line of fascia iliaca on top of it, and the abdominal wall muscles above. Sartorius sits superficial at the lower end of the image. The muscles above the fascia form a bow tie shape.
4. Colour Doppler finds the deep circumflex iliac artery, which runs just above the fascia and marks the level of the plane.
5. Insert the needle in plane from the lower end of the probe, aiming upwards under fascia iliaca.
6. Inject 2 to 3 mL and watch the fluid lift the fascia and run towards the head along iliacus. Then inject the rest.

## Reading the view

- **Iliacus.** A thick, dark muscle sloping into the pelvis.
- **Fascia iliaca.** The bright line on top of iliacus.
- **Correct spread.** A long strip of fluid under the fascia, running towards the head.
- **Wrong layer.** Fluid among the abdominal wall muscles or around sartorius.

> **The aha** Turn the probe sagittal. The long axis view lets you see the fluid travel up the plane, which is what makes this approach work.

> **Pitfall** Higher up, the bowel and peritoneum lie deep to the abdominal wall muscles, beyond the curve of the iliac fossa. Keep the needle tip on iliacus and in view, and never advance it past the curve of the pelvis.

?? Which probe orientation does the supra-inguinal approach use?
+ Sagittal, medial to the anterior superior iliac spine, above the inguinal ligament
- Transverse, below the inguinal ligament over the femoral artery
- Transverse over the pubic symphysis
- Parasagittal over the lumbar spine
: A sagittal view above the ligament shows iliacus, fascia iliaca and the spread towards the head in one image.

?? What advantage does the supra-inguinal approach have over the infra-inguinal approach?
+ More consistent spread to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve
- It needs no ultrasound
- It uses a much smaller volume
- It blocks the sciatic nerve
: Placing the injection above the ligament gives more reliable spread under the fascia to the femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves.

?? Which vessel helps confirm the plane in the supra-inguinal view?
+ The deep circumflex iliac artery
- The femoral vein
- The popliteal artery
- The inferior epigastric vein below the umbilicus
: The deep circumflex iliac artery runs in the abdominal wall just above fascia iliaca, so it marks the level of the target plane.

?? How does correct spread appear?
+ A long strip of fluid under fascia iliaca running towards the head
- Fluid in the fat over sartorius
- Fluid among the abdominal wall muscles
- A round swelling within iliacus
: The aim is long spread under the fascia along iliacus. Fluid in other layers means the tip is in the wrong place.

?? Why does this block still need a dose calculation, even though the needle stays away from nerves?
+ It uses 30 to 40 mL, which brings the dose close to the maximum for many patients
- The needle enters the femoral artery
- It needs adrenaline in every case
- It blocks both legs
: Plane blocks rely on volume, and volume means dose. Calculate the safe dose for the patient's weight.
