The jugular venous pressure (JVP) tells you how full the right side of the heart is. At the bedside it is often hard to see: a large neck, a noisy department, a patient who cannot lie still. Ultrasound shows you the internal jugular vein (IJV) directly, so you see where the column of blood ends.

![Wavelength Pearl: ultrasound JVP. Follow the right internal jugular vein up from the clavicle until it collapses. The collapse point marks the top of the venous column.](/learn/images/jvp-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Sit the patient at 30 to 45°, head supported, looking straight ahead or turned only slightly to the left. Turning the head further stretches and flattens the vein.
2. Use the linear probe on the right side of the neck, in transverse.
3. Use plenty of gel and almost no pressure. Let the probe rest on the gel.
4. Start just above the clavicle. Find the common carotid artery (round, thick-walled, pulsatile) and the IJV beside it, lateral and usually more superficial.
5. Slide the probe slowly up the neck, following the IJV.
6. Stop where the vein tapers and its walls meet. This is the collapse point, the top of the venous column.
7. Turn the probe to longitudinal over the vein to confirm. The vein looks like a tapering tube, closing at the top.

## Reading the view

- **Collapse point low in the neck, near the clavicle.** The venous column is short. The right-sided filling pressure is low or normal.
- **Collapse point high in the neck, or no collapse point below the jaw.** The column is tall. The right-sided filling pressure is likely high.
- **Estimate the height.** Measure the vertical height of the collapse point above the sternal angle, as you would for a clinical JVP.

> **The aha** Do not judge the vein by its size. Find where it stops. The top of the column is the JVP.

> **Pitfall** Probe pressure flattens the IJV and creates a false low collapse point. Lift the probe until it barely touches the gel, then look again.

## Beyond the finding

- Use the JVP with the rest of the picture: the heart, the lungs, the inferior vena cava and the patient.
- Positive-pressure ventilation, forceful breathing, severe tricuspid regurgitation and superior vena cava obstruction all change the vein and mislead the estimate.

?? What marks the top of the venous column when you scan the internal jugular vein?
+ The point where the vein tapers and its walls meet
- The point where the vein looks widest
- The level of the carotid bifurcation
- The point where the vein crosses the clavicle
: Below the top of the column, blood fills and distends the vein. Above it, the vein is empty and its walls meet. The collapse point is the ultrasound equivalent of the top of the visible pulsation in a clinical JVP. Width of the vein varies with position, probe pressure and breathing.

?? You find a collapse point just above the clavicle. Then you press a little harder with the probe and it seems to move higher. What explains this?
+ Probe pressure is collapsing the vein, giving a falsely low reading
- The JVP is rising during the scan
- The patient has a high JVP
- The carotid artery is compressing the vein
: The internal jugular vein is a low-pressure vessel. Even modest probe pressure closes it, which makes the column look shorter than it is. Rest the probe on the gel with almost no pressure before you judge the collapse point.

?? In a breathless patient sitting at 45°, the right internal jugular vein stays full right up to the angle of the jaw. What does this suggest?
+ Raised right-sided filling pressure
- Low right-sided filling pressure
- A normal finding at 45°
- Dehydration
: When the vein stays filled high into the neck, the column of blood above the right atrium is tall. This suggests raised right atrial pressure, as in a raised JVP on examination. Combine it with the heart, lungs and history before you act.

?? Why do you scan the right internal jugular vein rather than the left?
+ It runs in a straighter line to the right atrium
- It is always larger than the left
- It is further from the carotid artery
- It does not change with breathing
: The right internal jugular vein runs almost straight down through the brachiocephalic vein and superior vena cava into the right atrium. The left takes a longer path across the chest, where other structures compress it. This is the same reason a clinical JVP is examined on the right.

?? Which of these makes the ultrasound JVP less reliable?
+ Positive-pressure ventilation
- A patient sitting at 45°
- Using the linear probe
- Scanning in transverse first
: Positive-pressure ventilation raises pressure in the chest and fills the neck veins, so the collapse point no longer reflects right atrial pressure alone. Forceful breathing, severe tricuspid regurgitation and superior vena cava obstruction mislead in the same way. The other options are the standard technique.
