You see the vein, you get a flash, and the catheter will not advance. Pushing harder is the wrong answer. A catheter can fail to thread even when the needle tip is close to the lumen, and a blind push can dissect beside the vein and leave you with a swollen arm and no line.

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Enter the vein in short axis, which is the easiest view for finding the target and entering it.
2. If the catheter will not advance, stop. Keep the needle still.
3. Rotate the probe through 90 degrees to long axis, so you see the vein as a tube with the needle in it.
4. Look at three things: where the tip sits, whether the wall is tented or the tip is against the side wall, and whether a valve or a bend lies ahead.
5. Flatten your angle so the needle runs along the vein, not into its wall.
6. Advance the needle a few millimetres, so the tip is well inside the lumen and the catheter tip follows it.
7. Advance the catheter off the needle under vision. If it still meets resistance, stop and reassess.

## Reading the view

- **Tip at the side wall.** The bright tip touches the vein wall and the catheter cannot pass.
- **Catheter in lumen.** A bright line running along the centre of the black tube.
- **A valve or a bend.** A thin echogenic leaflet in the lumen, or a sudden turn of the vein.

> **The aha** Short axis tells you where the vein is. Long axis tells you why the catheter will not go in.

> **Pitfall** Do not keep pushing blindly. You may dissect beside the vein, or pass through the wall.

## When to stop

If the catheter will not pass after you have corrected the angle, take it out. Choose a different segment or a different vein, and follow your trust's policy on the number of attempts before you ask for help.

?? The needle tip is in the vein but the catheter will not advance. What do you do first?
+ Stop pushing, keep the needle still and rotate the probe to long axis
- Push the catheter harder
- Withdraw the needle completely and start again without looking
- Increase the probe pressure
: Long axis shows the tip position, any wall tenting, and valves or bends. Pushing blindly risks dissecting beside the vein.

?? Which finding in long axis explains a catheter that will not thread?
+ The tip lies against the side wall, or a valve or bend lies ahead
- A wide, straight vein with the tip in the centre
- A pulsating vessel under the needle
- A bright gel layer above the skin
: A tip against the wall, a valve or a sharp bend each block a catheter. A wide, straight lumen with the tip in the centre does not.

?? Why flatten the needle angle before you advance the catheter?
+ The needle then runs along the vein instead of into the wall
- It makes the vein larger
- It lets you skip the long-axis check
- It prevents backflow
: A shallower angle lines the needle up with the vein, so the catheter follows the lumen.

?? What is the main benefit of short axis in this procedure?
+ It is easiest for finding the vein and entering it
- It shows the catheter along its whole length
- It shows valves along the vein
- It removes the need for a tourniquet
: Short axis shows the vein as a circle with its neighbours, so you find the target and enter it. Long axis shows the catheter and the wall.
