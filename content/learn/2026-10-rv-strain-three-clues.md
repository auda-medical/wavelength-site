In a breathless or shocked patient, the right ventricle tells you whether the heart is under pressure from the lungs. One enlarged ventricle has many causes, acute and chronic. Combining a few findings gives a much stronger answer.

![Wavelength Pearl: RV strain. Combine RV enlargement, septal flattening and venous and lung findings.](/learn/images/rv-strain-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Start with the apical four-chamber view using the phased-array probe. Compare the right ventricle with the left at the base.
2. Move to the parasternal short-axis view at the level of the papillary muscles. Look at the shape of the left ventricle and the septum.
3. Measure the right ventricular free wall in the subcostal view if you can.
4. Add the IVC, compression of both legs, and the lungs.

## Reading the view

- **RV enlargement:** in the apical four-chamber view, the right ventricle is normally about two thirds of the left. A right ventricle as wide as the left, or wider, is dilated.
- **Septal flattening:** in short axis, the septum flattens and the left ventricle looks like a D.
- **Venous and lung clues:** a plethoric IVC, a non-compressible femoral or popliteal vein, and clear lungs with A-lines all make pulmonary embolism more likely.
- **Acute or chronic:** a thin right ventricular wall suggests an acute rise in pressure. A free wall over 5 mm suggests chronic pressure load, such as pulmonary hypertension or chronic lung disease.

> **The aha** One sign suggests. A pattern persuades.

> **Pitfall** Chronic pulmonary hypertension, COPD and other chronic right heart disease mimic acute strain. A normal scan does not exclude pulmonary embolism, because most patients with a small embolus have a normal right ventricle.

## Acting on it

In a shocked patient with suspected high-risk pulmonary embolism, RV dysfunction on bedside echo supports treatment when CT is not immediately possible. Follow your local pulmonary embolism pathway and involve senior and specialist teams early.

?? In the apical four-chamber view, what is the normal size of the right ventricle relative to the left?
+ About two thirds of the left ventricle or smaller
- Equal to the left ventricle
- Twice the left ventricle
- About one third of the left ventricle
: A normal right ventricle measures about two thirds of the left at the base, or less. Equal to or larger than the left counts as dilated.

?? What does a D-shaped left ventricle in parasternal short axis indicate?
+ Septal flattening from raised right ventricular pressure or volume
- Left ventricular hypertrophy
- A pericardial effusion
- Normal anatomy
: When pressure in the right ventricle rises, the septum flattens towards the left ventricle and its round cross-section becomes a D.

?? The right ventricle is dilated with a free wall of 8 mm. What does the wall thickness suggest?
+ Chronic pressure load
- Acute pulmonary embolism in a previously healthy heart
- Pericardial effusion
- Right ventricular infarction
: A previously normal right ventricle cannot generate high pressures quickly and stays thin-walled when it dilates. Over 5 mm suggests hypertrophy from chronic pressure load.

?? A breathless patient has a normal right ventricle on bedside echo. What do you conclude about pulmonary embolism?
+ It is not excluded
- It is excluded
- It is confirmed
- Only CT pulmonary angiography would show RV strain
: Many patients with pulmonary embolism, especially a small one, have a normal right ventricle. Use your clinical probability and the usual pathway.
