Procedural sedation for a dislocated shoulder ties up staff, a resuscitation bay and monitoring time. A superior trunk block numbs the shoulder while the patient stays awake. In one German emergency department, clinicians reduced 124 dislocated shoulders under low-volume superior trunk blocks of about 4 to 5 mL over six years. Every reduction succeeded, with no clinically relevant respiratory or haemodynamic complications reported. That study did not measure diaphragm function, so stay alert.

## Why the superior trunk

The phrenic nerve runs close to the C5 root on the front of anterior scalene. An interscalene block at the roots paralyses the diaphragm on that side in most patients. The superior trunk lies further from the phrenic nerve. In a 2026 meta-analysis of eight surgical trials, superior trunk blocks reduced complete hemidiaphragmatic paralysis by about 90% compared with interscalene blocks. The risk falls, but it does not reach zero.

## Before you block

1. Confirm the dislocation and check for an associated fracture.
2. Check the axillary nerve and the pulses, and record them.
3. Do not use this block in patients with severe respiratory disease or a known paralysed diaphragm on the other side.
4. Record consent, calculate a safe dose, attach monitoring and know where the lipid emulsion is.

## The scan

1. Sit the patient up, or lie them supine with the head turned away.
2. Place the linear probe transversely on the side of the neck at the level of the cricoid.
3. Find the roots as dark round circles stacked between anterior and middle scalene: C5, C6 and C7, the traffic lights.
4. Follow C5 and C6 down towards the clavicle until they merge into one oval: the superior trunk.
5. Insert the needle in plane from the lateral side, through middle scalene, and stop beside the trunk.
6. Inject in small aliquots and watch the fluid surround the trunk.

## Reading the view

- **Roots.** Dark round circles in a vertical stack between the scalene muscles.
- **Superior trunk.** The point where C5 and C6 join into one larger oval, before the suprascapular nerve leaves it.
- **Good spread.** A dark ring of fluid around the trunk.

> **The aha** Trace the roots, then use the smallest effective volume.

> **Pitfall** Small volume reduces phrenic involvement but does not remove it. Watch the patient's breathing after the block, and keep them in the department until you are satisfied. Never inject into a nerve: stop if the patient feels sharp pain or the injection needs high pressure.

?? Why does a superior trunk block affect the diaphragm less often than an interscalene block?
+ The superior trunk lies further from the phrenic nerve than the C5 root
- The superior trunk contains no sensory fibres
- The phrenic nerve runs inside the superior trunk
- Superior trunk blocks always use no local anaesthetic
: The phrenic nerve lies close to the C5 root on anterior scalene. Moving the injection down to the superior trunk increases the distance, so less local anaesthetic reaches the phrenic nerve.

?? How do you find the superior trunk on ultrasound?
+ Follow the C5 and C6 roots down the neck until they join into one oval
- Find the subclavian artery and inject below it
- Scan the axilla for the axillary artery
- Locate the vertebral artery at C7
: The C5 and C6 roots sit between the scalene muscles. Tracing them towards the clavicle shows the point where they merge into the superior trunk.

?? Which patient should not have a superior trunk block?
+ A patient with a known paralysed diaphragm on the other side
- A patient with a first anterior dislocation
- A patient who wants to avoid sedation
- A patient with a normal neurovascular examination
: Even a low-volume superior trunk block can affect the phrenic nerve on the injected side. With the other side already paralysed, the patient could lose both diaphragms.

?? What did an emergency department study of low-volume superior trunk blocks for shoulder reduction report?
+ All reductions succeeded and no clinically relevant respiratory complications were reported, but diaphragm function was not measured
- Most patients needed sedation as well
- The block failed in half of the patients
- Diaphragm paralysis occurred in every patient
: In 124 blocks of about 4 to 5 mL, every reduction succeeded. The authors did not measure diaphragm function, so the true phrenic effect in this setting remains uncertain.

?? During injection the patient feels sharp, shooting pain down the arm. What do you do?
+ Stop injecting and reposition the needle
- Inject faster to finish the block
- Increase the volume
- Ask the patient to hold still and carry on
: Sharp pain or high injection pressure suggests the needle tip is in or against the nerve. Stop, withdraw slightly and reposition before injecting again.
