Achilles ruptures often present as a kick in the back of the heel during a sprint or a jump. Patients can still walk and plantarflex using the other calf and foot muscles, so the injury is often missed at first. The calf squeeze test is the key examination. Ultrasound adds the gap, its size and its position.

![Wavelength Pearl: Achilles rupture. If the still image is unclear, squeeze the calf or ask for plantarflexion. Movement reveals the gap.](/learn/images/achilles-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Lie the patient prone with both feet hanging over the end of the bed.
2. Use the high-frequency linear probe in long axis, with plenty of gel.
3. Start at the insertion on the calcaneus and scan up to the calf muscles.
4. Turn 90° and scan in short axis along the same length.
5. Squeeze the calf, or gently plantarflex and dorsiflex the foot, while you watch the tendon in long axis.
6. Compare with the other side.

## Reading the view

- **Normal.** A continuous, bright, fibrillar band. When you squeeze the calf, the whole tendon slides as one.
- **Complete rupture.** A gap filled with dark fluid or mixed haematoma, often a few centimetres above the insertion. The torn ends look frayed. Fat from behind the tendon can bulge into the gap. Movement above the gap does not pass to the tendon below.
- **Partial tear or tendinopathy.** A thickened, darker tendon with some continuous fibres. Movement passes through.

> **The aha** When the image hesitates, add movement. A tendon which moves as one is continuous.

> **Pitfall** The thin plantaris tendon often survives a rupture and runs along the medial side. Do not mistake it for intact Achilles fibres. Anisotropy also darkens normal fibres. Keep the beam at 90° before you call a gap.

?? Which dynamic test helps confirm a complete Achilles rupture on ultrasound?
+ Squeezing the calf while watching the tendon in long axis
- Asking the patient to stand on tiptoe
- Applying colour Doppler over the tendon
- Pressing firmly on the heel with the probe
: Squeezing the calf normally moves the whole tendon. In a complete rupture, the movement stops at the gap and does not pass to the tendon below.

?? A patient can still plantarflex the ankle after an injury. What does this mean?
+ It does not exclude an Achilles rupture
- It excludes an Achilles rupture
- It proves a partial tear
- It means the plantaris tendon has torn
: Tibialis posterior, the peroneal muscles and the long toe flexors can still plantarflex the foot. Rely on the calf squeeze test and imaging, not active plantarflexion.

?? A thin, intact tendon runs along the medial side of a gap in the Achilles. What is it most likely to be?
+ The plantaris tendon
- Intact Achilles fibres
- The sural nerve
- The posterior tibial artery
: The plantaris tendon is thin and runs medial to the Achilles. It often survives a rupture and can be mistaken for continuous Achilles fibres.

?? Where does an Achilles rupture most often occur?
+ A few centimetres above the insertion on the calcaneus
- At the musculotendinous junction high in the calf
- Within the calcaneus
- At the knee
: Most ruptures occur in the middle portion of the tendon, a few centimetres above the calcaneal insertion, where the blood supply is poorest.

?? How do you avoid mistaking anisotropy for a tear in the Achilles tendon?
+ Keep the beam at 90° to the fibres and check in two planes
- Increase the gain until the tendon is bright
- Scan only in short axis
- Use the curvilinear probe
: Tendons look dark when the beam meets them at an angle. Correcting the angle and confirming in long and short axis separates anisotropy from a true gap.
