The front of the abdominal wall around the midline receives its supply from the anterior branches of the lower thoracic nerves. These run between rectus abdominis and the posterior rectus sheath before turning forward through the muscle. A rectus sheath block places local anaesthetic in this space. It treats pain from the abdominal wall, not from inside the abdomen. Most of the evidence comes from operating theatres, for midline and umbilical incisions.

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## Before you block

1. Be sure the pain comes from the abdominal wall. A block can hide the signs of a problem inside the abdomen.
2. Record consent, calculate the maximum safe dose (a midline wound needs both sides), attach monitoring and know where the lipid emulsion is.

## The scan

1. Lie the patient supine.
2. Place the linear probe transversely just lateral to the umbilicus, above the arcuate line.
3. Identify rectus abdominis, an oval muscle between the bright anterior and posterior sheaths, and the peritoneum deep to the posterior sheath.
4. Use colour Doppler to find the inferior epigastric vessels, which run on the back of the muscle.
5. Insert the needle in plane from the lateral side, through the muscle, until the tip lies just above the posterior sheath.
6. Inject 1 to 2 mL and watch the fluid separate the muscle from the posterior sheath. Then inject the rest. Repeat on the other side for a midline wound.

## Reading the view

- **Rectus abdominis.** An oval muscle between two bright lines.
- **Posterior rectus sheath.** The deeper bright line. The peritoneum lies just beneath it.
- **Correct spread.** A dark strip of fluid between the back of the muscle and the posterior sheath.
- **Wrong layer.** A round swelling within the muscle.

> **The aha** The posterior sheath is your floor. Fluid peeling the muscle off it confirms the plane.

> **Pitfall** Below the arcuate line, about a third of the way from the umbilicus to the pubis, the posterior sheath ends and only thin transversalis fascia separates you from the peritoneum. Block above the arcuate line and keep the tip in view.

?? Where does the needle tip sit in a rectus sheath block?
+ Between rectus abdominis and the posterior rectus sheath
- In the subcutaneous fat over the anterior sheath
- Inside the peritoneal cavity
- Between external and internal oblique
: The anterior branches of the lower thoracic nerves run in this space before turning forward through the muscle.

?? What kind of pain does a rectus sheath block treat?
+ Pain from the abdominal wall, not from inside the abdomen
- Pain from the bowel and other abdominal organs
- Pain from the kidney
- Pain from the lumbar spine
: The block reaches the nerves to the skin and muscle of the central abdominal wall. It does not treat pain from inside the abdomen.

?? Why do you block above the arcuate line?
+ Below it the posterior sheath ends, leaving only thin fascia over the peritoneum
- Below it the rectus muscle is absent
- Above it the block lasts longer
- The epigastric vessels are only present below it
: Above the arcuate line, the posterior sheath gives a firm floor for the injection.

?? Which vessels run on the back of rectus abdominis and need colour Doppler?
+ The inferior epigastric vessels
- The femoral vessels
- The internal mammary vessels
- The deep circumflex iliac vessels
: The inferior epigastric vessels run on the deep surface of the muscle. Colour Doppler helps you avoid them.

?? A patient has pain from a midline wound. What do you need?
+ A block on both sides, with a dose calculation which covers both
- A block on one side only
- A single injection in the midline
- No dose calculation, as the volumes are small
: Each side of the midline has its own nerve supply. Two injections double the total dose, so calculate the safe dose first.
