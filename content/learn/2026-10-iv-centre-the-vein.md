Most failed ultrasound-guided cannulations start before the needle touches the skin. The vein sits at the edge of the screen, or it is flattened by the probe, or the scanner has not checked that the target stays in view as the probe moves. Fixing the picture first costs a few seconds and saves attempts.

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Apply a tourniquet and scan the limb in short axis with a high-frequency linear probe. Find the vein and the artery beside it.
2. Slide the probe until the target vein sits in the middle of the screen, under the centre of the probe.
3. Reduce the pressure until the vein is round. A flattened vein is smaller than it really is.
4. Set the depth so the vein fills the middle of the screen, with the skin and the deeper structures still in view.
5. Rest the heel of your scanning hand on the patient, so small movements do not shift the image.
6. Run the dynamic check: slide a short way along the vein, then back, and keep the same vein centred and round. If you cannot do this, you will not be able to follow the needle either.
7. Puncture only when the target stays round and stable.

## Reading the view

- **Good target.** A round, black, compressible vein in the middle of the screen, with the artery clearly separate.
- **Poor target.** A vein at the screen edge, or a flat slit under heavy pressure. The needle path is harder to predict and threading is harder.

> **The aha** Slide before you stab. Moving the probe a few centimetres along the vein costs nothing, and the vein that stays centred and round is the one to use.

> **Pitfall** If the vessel sits at the edge of the screen or collapses under probe pressure, you are lining up a failure before you start.

## When ultrasound helps most

A 2022 Cochrane review found that ultrasound guidance probably improves first-pass success in adults with moderately difficult access and may improve it in those with difficult access. In patients with easy access, first-pass success was lower with ultrasound, and the procedure took longer. Use ultrasound when the vein is hard to see or feel, not as a replacement for landmark cannulation in every patient.

?? Why do you centre the vein on the screen before puncture?
+ A centred, round vein gives a predictable needle path to the target
- It makes the vein look larger than it is
- It removes the need to look at the needle
- It lets you use a lower frequency
: In short axis the vein at the centre of the screen lies under the centre of the probe. The needle path to it is then easier to predict, and you can follow the tip as it advances.

?? What is the dynamic check before puncture?
+ Slide a short way along the vein and back, keeping the same vein centred and round
- Press hard to see whether the vein disappears
- Tilt the probe until the artery is visible
- Ask the patient to clench the fist
: If you cannot keep the target centred and open while you move the probe, you will not be able to track it while you steer the needle.

?? The vein sits at the edge of the screen and looks flat. What do you do first?
+ Slide to centre it, reduce the pressure until it is round, then recheck
- Puncture now, as the vein is shallow
- Increase the pressure to hold the image still
- Switch to the artery as the target
: A flat vein is a vein under pressure. Centre it and let it fill before you judge its size.

?? In which patients does ultrasound guidance help most for peripheral cannulation, according to the 2022 Cochrane review?
+ Adults with moderately difficult or difficult access
- Adults with easily seen veins
- Every patient, by the same amount
- Only children
: The review found a benefit for first-pass success in difficult and moderately difficult patients. In easy patients the first-pass rate was lower with ultrasound, and the procedure took longer.
