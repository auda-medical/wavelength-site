Judging left ventricular (LV) function by eye takes practice. E-point septal separation (EPSS) gives new scanners a number to anchor that judgement. It measures how close the anterior mitral leaflet comes to the interventricular septum when the valve opens in early diastole.

A well-filled, strongly contracting ventricle drives a brisk opening, and the leaflet almost touches the septum. A dilated, poorly contracting ventricle fills slowly, and the leaflet falls short.

## The scan

1. Use the phased-array probe and the cardiac preset.
2. Get a parasternal long-axis (PLAX) view at the left sternal border, third or fourth intercostal space.
3. Optimise the view: the septum and the posterior wall lie horizontal, and the mitral valve and aortic valve both open on screen.
4. Switch on M-mode and place the line through the tip of the anterior mitral leaflet.
5. On the M-mode trace, the leaflet makes an M shape. The first, taller peak is the E-point.
6. Measure from the E-point to the septum at that moment.

## Reading the view

- **Normal.** The E-point comes within about 7 mm of the septum.
- **Increased EPSS.** A gap above about 7 mm supports reduced LV systolic function. The wider the gap, the weaker the ventricle tends to be.
- **No M-mode?** Freeze the PLAX loop, scroll to the frame where the leaflet opens widest in early diastole, and measure the shortest distance from the leaflet tip to the septum.

> **The aha** A strong heart throws the mitral leaflet at the septum. Measure the gap, then look at the whole ventricle.

> **Pitfall** Anything which stops the leaflet moving freely makes EPSS read high without poor LV function: mitral stenosis, a regurgitant aortic jet hitting the anterior leaflet, or an off-axis view. A thick septum makes it read falsely low.

## Beyond the number

- EPSS supports your visual estimate of LV function. It does not replace it, and it is not an ejection fraction.
- Check the rest of the heart: the right ventricle, the pericardium and the valves, then the lungs and the inferior vena cava.

?? Where do you place the M-mode line to measure EPSS?
+ Through the tip of the anterior mitral leaflet in the parasternal long-axis view
- Through the aortic valve in the parasternal short-axis view
- Through the mitral annulus in the apical four-chamber view
- Through the right ventricle in the subcostal view
: EPSS measures how close the anterior mitral leaflet comes to the septum. The parasternal long-axis view shows both in line with the beam, and M-mode through the leaflet tip records the E-point clearly. Measurements at the annulus or in other views answer different questions.

?? What does the E-point represent on the M-mode trace of the mitral valve?
+ The maximum opening of the anterior leaflet in early diastole
- The closure of the mitral valve at the start of systole
- The opening of the valve during atrial contraction
- The peak of the ventricular contraction
: In sinus rhythm the anterior leaflet opens twice in diastole: first with early passive filling, the E-point, then with atrial contraction, the A-point. The E-point is the taller first peak and the one you measure to the septum.

?? An adult has an EPSS of 14 mm. What does this suggest, if the view is good and the valves are normal?
+ Reduced left ventricular systolic function
- Normal left ventricular function
- A pericardial effusion
- Hypovolaemia
: A gap above about 7 mm supports reduced LV systolic function, and 14 mm is well above it. In a hypovolaemic, hyperdynamic heart the leaflet often comes very close to the septum. Confirm with a visual estimate of the whole ventricle.

?? Which condition makes EPSS read high even when left ventricular function is normal?
+ Aortic regurgitation, with the jet hitting the anterior mitral leaflet
- Sinus tachycardia
- A small pericardial effusion
- Hypovolaemia
: A regurgitant aortic jet strikes the anterior mitral leaflet in diastole and stops it opening fully, so the gap to the septum widens. Mitral stenosis does the same by restricting the leaflet. In both, EPSS overestimates LV impairment.

?? How should you use EPSS in a breathless patient?
+ As one objective clue alongside a visual assessment of the whole ventricle
- As a direct measure of ejection fraction
- As the only cardiac measurement needed
- Only in patients with mitral stenosis
: EPSS is quick and objective, which makes it useful for new scanners. It reflects LV function indirectly and is affected by valve disease, septal thickness and the view. Combine it with what the whole ventricle is doing, and with the rest of the scan.
