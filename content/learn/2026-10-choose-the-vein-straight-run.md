A puncture that succeeds and a catheter that will not thread usually means the wrong segment, not the wrong technique. Hidden branches, valves and sharp turns stop the catheter. A deep vein holds only the tip of a short catheter, which then falls out. A minute spent surveying the arm picks a vein that works first time.

## The scan

1. Apply a tourniquet. Scan the forearm and upper arm in short axis, moving proximally and distally.
2. Map the run of each candidate vein: where it branches, turns or dives deep.
3. Compress each candidate to prove it is a vein and free of clot.
4. Turn to long axis over the best candidate. Look for a straight run, with no valve or branch, over the length of your catheter.
5. Measure the depth from the skin to the top of the vein.
6. Choose the segment which is straight, compressible, superficial enough and easy to follow.

## Depth and catheter length

The catheter needs enough length inside the vein to stay there. A 2020 emergency department study found that peripheral catheters lasted longer when more than 2.75 cm of the catheter lay inside the vein. A standard short cannula through 1.5 cm of tissue leaves little in the lumen. For a deeper vein, choose a longer catheter if your trust stocks one, or pick a more superficial vein.

## Reading the view

- **Good run.** A straight, black tube in long axis, compressible, with a clear wall.
- **Poor run.** A valve leaflet in the lumen, a side branch, or a tortuous turn.
- **Size versus depth.** A large vein 15 mm deep is often harder than a smaller one 5 mm deep.

> **The aha** Pick the straight run, not the nearest circle. Short axis shows you a vein. Long axis shows you whether a catheter will fit in it.

> **Pitfall** A branch, valve or sharp turn makes cannulation fail even when the puncture succeeds.

## Nerves and arteries

In the upper arm, the basilic vein runs near the brachial artery and the median nerve. Know what lies beside and beneath your target before you choose it.

?? Which segment makes the best target for a peripheral catheter?
+ A straight, compressible, superficial segment with enough length for the catheter
- The largest vein, whatever its depth
- The first round vein seen in short axis
- A vein at a branch point, where it is widest
: The catheter needs a straight run to thread and enough length in the lumen to stay in. Size matters less than depth and path.

?? The puncture succeeds but the catheter will not thread. What is a likely cause?
+ A valve, branch or sharp turn in the segment
- The vein is too superficial
- The tourniquet is on
- The vein is compressible
: Hidden branches and valves often explain easy puncture with failed threading. Survey the run before you puncture.

?? Why can a deep vein lead to early catheter failure?
+ Too little of the catheter lies inside the vein
- Deep veins are always thrombosed
- Deep veins never compress
- Deep veins have no valves
: A short catheter through thick tissue leaves only its tip in the lumen. More catheter in the vein lasts longer.

?? How do you check the run of a candidate vein?
+ Scan along it in short axis, then look at it in long axis
- Look at the skin surface only
- Use colour Doppler at one point
- Press hard at the puncture site only
: Scanning along the vein shows branches and turns, and long axis shows valves and the length of a straight segment.
