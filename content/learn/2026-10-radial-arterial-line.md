An arterial line in a shocked patient is hard to place by palpation, because the pulse is weak and the artery is small. Ultrasound shows the artery, its walls and its neighbours, so you can choose the easiest stretch instead of the first pulse you feel. A 2014 meta-analysis of randomised trials found that ultrasound guidance improves the chance of success at the first attempt and reduces the number of attempts for radial artery catheterisation.

## Before you start

Record consent where possible, check the hand is well perfused and choose the limb according to your trust's policy. Use sterile technique and local anaesthetic in the awake patient.

## The scan

1. Support the wrist on a roll or folded towel, in gentle extension. Over-extension can flatten the artery, so stop short of the end of range. Keep the hand still.
2. Scan the distal forearm in short axis with a high-frequency linear probe. The radial artery is a round, pulsating vessel, close to a tendon and usually with a vein on each side.
3. Slide up and down the forearm a few centimetres. Choose a straight, superficial segment with a clear lumen and stable wall, away from branches and bends.
4. Enter at a low angle. A shallow angle gives better control of the tip and an easier catheter entry.
5. When you see pulsatile blood return, lower the angle and advance, then thread the catheter under vision.
6. If the puncture succeeds but the catheter will not advance, rotate to long axis and look at the tip and the wall, as for a peripheral line.

## Reading the view

- **Radial artery.** A round, pulsating, non-compressible lumen with a bright wall.
- **Tendon.** A bright, fibrillar structure beside the artery that does not pulsate.
- **Bifurcation or branch.** Two lumens in one segment. Move to a straight stretch.

> **The aha** Scan a few centimetres before you choose. The easiest segment is often not where the pulse is strongest.

> **Pitfall** Puncturing at a bifurcation or over a mobile wrist segment turns a simple line into a difficult one.

## After the attempt

After you remove a catheter or a failed needle, apply firm pressure over the site until bleeding stops, and check the hand's colour and warmth. Watch for haematoma and for any change in perfusion.

?? How does ultrasound guidance change radial artery catheterisation, according to a 2014 meta-analysis of randomised trials?
+ It improves first-attempt success and reduces the number of attempts
- It removes the need for local anaesthetic
- It worsens first-attempt success
- It has no effect on attempts
: The meta-analysis pooled randomised trials and found better first-attempt success and fewer attempts with ultrasound guidance.

?? How do you choose the segment of radial artery to puncture?
+ Scan a few centimetres and choose a straight, superficial stretch with a clear lumen
- Choose the point where you feel the strongest pulse
- Choose the bifurcation, as the artery is largest there
- Choose the wrist crease every time
: A straight stretch without branches or bends gives the catheter room to thread. The strongest pulse is not always the best segment.

?? Why enter at a low angle?
+ It gives better control of the needle tip and easier catheter entry
- It makes the artery larger
- It avoids the need for a wrist roll
- It prevents flashback
: A shallow angle keeps the needle running with the artery. A steep angle risks passing through the back wall.

?? What do you do if the puncture succeeds but the catheter will not advance?
+ Rotate to long axis and look at the tip and the wall
- Push the catheter harder
- Apply pressure and give up
- Remove the roll and extend the wrist fully
: Long axis shows the angle, tip position and any wall tenting. Pushing blindly can dissect the artery.
