Fluid helps some patients and harms others. Venous congestion, where pressure backs up from the right heart into the organs, is one sign that more fluid will harm. The portal vein shows this pressure because the liver sits between it and the heart.

## The scan

1. Use the curvilinear probe, abdominal preset.
2. Find the portal vein at the liver hilum, from below the right costal margin or through the right intercostal spaces. It runs towards the liver with bright walls.
3. Confirm flow with colour Doppler.
4. Place the pulsed-wave Doppler gate inside the portal vein, with the beam as parallel to the flow as you can get it.
5. Record several seconds, and save the trace.

## Reading the trace

- **Normal.** Continuous flow towards the liver, with little variation.
- **Mild pulsatility.** Regular undulations in velocity with each heartbeat.
- **Marked pulsatility.** Large swings in velocity, often reaching or crossing the baseline, so flow briefly stops or reverses.

Pulsatility fraction = (maximum velocity minus minimum velocity) divided by maximum velocity. Under 30% is normal. 30% to 50% is mildly abnormal. 50% or more is severely abnormal.

> **The aha** Steady portal flow means pressure from the right heart is not reaching the liver. A pulsing portal vein suggests it is.

> **Pitfall** Portal vein pulsatility is not a fluid-responsiveness test. Interpret it with the IVC, hepatic vein and intrarenal venous Doppler (the VExUS approach) and the clinical context.

## Other causes of a pulsatile portal vein

Healthy, thin young adults sometimes show a pulsatile portal vein. Tricuspid regurgitation and arteriovenous shunts in the liver also cause it. Cirrhosis stiffens the liver and damps the signal, so congestion is harder to see.

?? Normal portal vein flow on pulsed-wave Doppler is best described as:
+ Continuous flow towards the liver with little variation
- Flow reversing with each heartbeat
- Absent flow at rest
- Flow away from the liver
: The liver buffers the pressure waves of the right heart, so normal portal flow is steady and directed towards the liver. Reversal or stopping with each beat suggests raised right-sided pressure. Flow away from the liver suggests portal hypertension.

?? What does marked portal vein pulsatility suggest?
+ Raised right-sided venous pressure, consistent with venous congestion
- Fluid responsiveness
- Low cardiac output with dehydration
- Portal vein thrombosis
: Pressure from the right heart reaches the portal vein when the right side is congested. A pulsing portal vein is a congestion clue, not a test of whether more fluid will help. Portal vein thrombosis shows absent flow or filling defects.

?? A portal vein has a maximum velocity of 20 cm/s and a minimum of 8 cm/s. What is the pulsatility fraction?
+ 60%
- 40%
- 28%
- 12%
: Pulsatility fraction = (20 minus 8) divided by 20 = 0.6, or 60%. A value of 50% or more is severely abnormal and suggests significant venous congestion, to interpret alongside the rest of the VExUS assessment.

?? Which approach combines the IVC with hepatic, portal and intrarenal venous Doppler?
+ VExUS
- eFAST
- RUSH
- BLUE protocol
: VExUS (venous excess ultrasound) grades venous congestion using the IVC and Doppler of the hepatic vein, portal vein and intrarenal veins. eFAST looks for free fluid and pneumothorax in trauma. The BLUE protocol assesses lung in breathlessness.

?? Which patient might show a pulsatile portal vein without congestion?
+ A healthy, thin young adult
- A patient with cirrhosis
- A patient with portal vein thrombosis
- A patient with a small spleen
: Some healthy, thin young adults show portal pulsatility without raised right-sided pressure. Cirrhosis stiffens the liver and damps pulsatility. Portal vein thrombosis reduces or abolishes flow.
