The vesicoureteric junction is one of the narrowest points in the ureter, so stones often lodge there. Most of the ureter is hidden by bowel gas, but its lowest part lies just behind the bladder. Fill the bladder and you have a window onto it, which is easy to forget when the scan has stopped at the kidney.

![Wavelength Pearl: stone at the bladder. A smooth bladder back wall with a ureteric jet, a bright echo with a shadow where the ureter enters, and a twinkle behind the stone.](/learn/images/vuj-stone-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Start with a reasonably full bladder. Ask the patient to drink and come back if it is empty.
2. Use the curvilinear probe above the pubic bone, angled down to the back wall.
3. Look at the back wall on each side, in transverse and then sagittal.
4. Follow any dilated ureter down to the bladder.
5. Add colour Doppler, watch for [ureteric jets](/learn/renal-colic-ultrasound-ureteric-jets/), and look for a [twinkle](/learn/renal-stone-twinkle-artefact/) behind any bright echo.
6. Scan both kidneys for [hydronephrosis](/learn/hydronephrosis-ultrasound/).

## Reading the view

- **Normal:** a smooth back wall, and a jet of urine from each side.
- **Stone at the junction:** a bright echo with a shadow where the ureter enters.
- **Supporting signs:** a dilated lower ureter, a twinkle, or no jet on the painful side after a few minutes of watching.
- **Stone loose in the bladder:** it rolls when the patient turns. A stone in the junction stays where it is.
- **Bladder mass or clot:** a [filling defect](/learn/bladder-clot-ultrasound/) does not shadow in the same way, and a tumour may have flow in it.

> **The aha** Fill the bladder. Then look behind it.

> **Pitfall** A normal scan does not exclude a ureteric stone. Fever with obstruction is a urological emergency. Twinkle is a clue, not proof, because other calcification and strong interfaces can twinkle too.

## Action

Look for hydronephrosis on that side. Low-dose CT remains the usual test for a suspected stone in adults, where the answer changes care, and ultrasound comes first in pregnancy and in children. Fever or sepsis with a suspected stone needs urgent urology, as do a single kidney, a kidney injury or uncontrolled pain. An older patient with a first episode of renal colic needs the aorta checked too.

?? Why does a full bladder help?
+ It gives a clear window onto the lowest part of the ureter
- It moves the kidneys closer to the probe
- It removes the need for colour Doppler
- It makes stones dissolve
: Urine carries sound well, and it pushes the bowel out of the way.

?? Which finding at the back of the bladder suggests a stone at the junction?
+ A bright echo with a shadow where the ureter enters
- A smooth wall with a jet of urine on each side
- A round black balloon
- A dark layer of fluid behind the uterus
: Add a dilated ureter or an absent jet on that side, and the picture is stronger.

?? A bright echo at the back of the bladder rolls away when the patient turns. What is it most likely to be?
+ A stone loose in the bladder
- A stone stuck at the vesicoureteric junction
- A ureteric jet
- A bladder tumour
: A stone in the junction is fixed. A loose bladder stone moves with gravity.

?? A bright echo at the junction twinkles on colour Doppler. How much does this tell you?
+ It supports a stone, but it is a clue and not proof
- It proves a stone, so no other test is ever needed
- It excludes a stone
- It shows active bleeding
: Other calcification and strong interfaces can twinkle too.

?? The scan shows a stone at the junction, with hydronephrosis, and the patient has a fever. What do you do?
+ Call urology urgently: an infected, obstructed kidney is an emergency
- Discharge with analgesia and a sieve
- Book a routine scan in six weeks
- Ask the patient to drink more and return tomorrow
: Fever with obstruction needs antibiotics and urgent decompression.
