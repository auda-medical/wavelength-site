Tendons are bright and fibrillar on ultrasound only when the beam meets them at 90°. Tilt the probe a few degrees and a healthy tendon turns dark. This is anisotropy, the most common trap in musculoskeletal ultrasound.

![Wavelength Pearl: tendon tear or anisotropy. Heel-toe the probe until the fibres brighten.](/learn/images/tendon-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Use the high-frequency linear probe with plenty of gel.
2. Scan the tendon in long axis, then turn 90° to short axis.
3. Where the tendon curves, rock the probe along its length: press the heel of the probe, then the toe, until the fibres turn bright.
4. In short axis, tilt the probe from side to side for the same reason.
5. Compare with the other side.
6. Move the joint gently and watch the tendon glide.

## Reading the view

- **Normal.** Bright, parallel fibres in long axis, and a bright speckled oval in short axis.
- **Anisotropy.** The tendon turns dark when the beam is oblique, and bright again when you correct the angle.
- **Tear.** Fibres break, leaving a dark gap or a defect which stays whatever the angle. In a full tear the ends separate, and movement of the joint shows they do not glide together.

> **The aha** If the darkness disappears when you heel-toe, it was anisotropy.

> **Pitfall** Do not diagnose a tear from one dark longitudinal image. Check in two planes, correct the angle, and compare with the other side.

## Use anisotropy to your advantage

A nerve and a tendon look alike in short axis. Tilt the probe. The tendon darkens with a small change in angle, while the nerve changes little. This helps when you scan the wrist or before a nerve block.

?? Why does a normal tendon look dark when the probe is tilted?
+ The beam hits the fibres at an angle, so less sound returns to the probe
- Fluid collects around the tendon when it is compressed
- The tendon is torn
- The gain is set too low
: Tendon fibres reflect sound like a mirror. When the beam meets them at 90°, the echoes return to the probe and the tendon looks bright. At an angle, the echoes reflect away and the tendon looks dark. This is anisotropy.

?? A tendon has a dark area in long axis. You heel-toe the probe and the fibres turn bright. What is the diagnosis?
+ Anisotropy in a normal tendon
- Partial tendon tear
- Full tendon tear
- Tendinopathy with fluid
: A dark area which turns bright when you correct the beam angle is anisotropy. A tear shows a defect which stays dark whatever the angle.

?? Which finding supports a full tendon tear?
+ Separated tendon ends which do not glide together when the joint moves
- A dark area which brightens with heel-toe
- Bright, parallel fibres in long axis
- A speckled oval in short axis
: In a full tear, the fibres are discontinuous, the ends separate, and gentle movement shows the ends do not move together. The other findings describe a normal tendon or anisotropy.

?? How do you use anisotropy to tell a tendon from a nerve in short axis?
+ Tilt the probe: the tendon darkens, the nerve changes little
- Apply colour Doppler: the nerve shows flow
- Press firmly: the nerve collapses
- Increase the depth: the tendon disappears
: Tendons are far more anisotropic than nerves. A small tilt makes a tendon go dark while a nerve stays visible. Colour Doppler and compression do not reliably separate them.

?? Which step reduces the risk of over-calling a tear?
+ Scan in two planes and compare with the other side
- Use the curvilinear probe
- Increase the gain until the tendon looks bright
- Scan only in long axis
: Two planes and a comparison with the other side separate a true defect from anisotropy and normal variation. Increasing the gain does not correct the beam angle. The curvilinear probe gives less detail of superficial tendons.
