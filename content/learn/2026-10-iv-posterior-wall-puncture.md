The commonest way to lose a good vein is to go through it. The needle enters the lumen, the operator pushes on, and the tip passes through the back wall. A flash of blood can still appear, because the bevel can sit partly in the lumen and partly outside it. The catheter then fails to thread, or the vein blows and the patient has a bruise.

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## The scan

1. Scan the vein in short axis. Keep it centred and round with light pressure.
2. Approach at a low angle, so the tip meets the near wall gently.
3. Follow the tip as it advances. Slide the probe a little after the needle so the tip stays in view.
4. When the tip reaches the near wall, slow down. The wall tents, which tells you the tip has arrived.
5. Advance a fraction more, until the tip is inside the lumen. Then stop.
6. Confirm by image, not by flash, before you advance the catheter.

## If you overshoot

1. Stop and keep the probe still, with the tip in view.
2. Withdraw slowly under vision until the tip is back inside the lumen.
3. Lower the angle and re-enter.
4. Check the tip position again before you advance the catheter.

## Reading the view

- **Near-wall tenting.** The wall bends in toward the lumen under the needle tip. It is a sign the tip is at the wall, not in the vein.
- **Tip in lumen.** A bright dot inside a black, open lumen.
- **Through the back wall.** The bright tip lies beyond the posterior wall, even though blood is still returning.

> **The aha** The image matters more than the flash. If you cannot see the tip inside the lumen, you do not know where it is.

> **Pitfall** You can still get flashback after the needle has gone through the back wall.

## Why it matters at the elbow

In the antecubital fossa and the upper arm, an artery or a nerve often lies beneath the vein. A needle that passes through the back wall can injure it. Take extra care to see the tip and depth in these areas.

?? What does tenting of the near wall tell you?
+ The needle tip has reached the wall and has not yet entered the lumen
- The needle is in the artery
- The needle is through the back wall
- The vein is thrombosed
: The wall bends under the tip as it presses on it. Advance a little more to enter, then stop.

?? Why is flashback unreliable for confirming the needle is in the lumen?
+ Blood can return when the bevel sits partly in the lumen after the needle has passed through the back wall
- Flashback never occurs in veins
- Flashback only occurs in arteries
- Flashback is a sign of tenting
: A needle through both walls can still return blood if part of the bevel lies in the lumen. Rely on the image of the tip.

?? You have gone through the back wall. What do you do?
+ Stop, withdraw slowly under vision until the tip is in the lumen, lower the angle and re-enter
- Advance the catheter straight away
- Remove the probe and continue by feel
- Push the needle further and withdraw quickly
: Retrace the path under vision. Re-enter at a lower angle, and check the tip is inside the lumen before you advance the catheter.

?? Why is extra care needed over the vein in the antecubital fossa?
+ An artery or nerve often lies beneath the vein, and a needle through the back wall can injure it
- The veins there are always thrombosed
- The vein is too small to see
- Ultrasound cannot be used there
: A needle that transfixes the vein can reach structures beneath it. Keep the tip in view and stop once it is in the lumen.
