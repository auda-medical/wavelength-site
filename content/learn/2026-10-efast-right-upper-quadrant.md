The right upper quadrant view is the most sensitive single view in the FAST scan for free intraperitoneal fluid. Many clinicians stop at Morison's pouch, the space between the liver and the right kidney. Small volumes of fluid often appear first further down, at the inferior tip of the liver and around the lower pole of the kidney.

![Wavelength Pearl: eFAST right upper quadrant. Scan the whole view and look at the inferior tip of the liver.](/learn/images/efast-pearl.webp)

*Scan images in the graphic are illustrations, not patient scans.*

## How to scan it

1. Place the curvilinear probe in the mid to posterior axillary line, around the 8th to 11th intercostal space, with the marker towards the head.
2. Find the liver and the right kidney, then identify the hepatorenal space.
3. Fan through the whole space, front to back.
4. Slide down until you see the inferior tip of the liver and the lower pole of the kidney.
5. Slide up to see the diaphragm and the space above it for haemothorax.

## What fluid looks like

Free fluid appears as a black, sharp-edged stripe. It follows the shape of the space it fills, with angled corners rather than a round outline. A round black structure with a smooth wall is more likely the gallbladder, a cyst, the IVC or fluid-filled bowel.

> **The aha** Fluid takes the shape of its container. Angled corners mean free fluid. A round outline means a structure.

## Why a negative scan does not rule out injury

FAST is specific but misses injuries. A negative scan lowers the chance of a large bleed at that moment. It does not exclude intra-abdominal injury.

- **Small volumes.** Early in the course of a bleed, the volume of fluid sits below what the scan detects.
- **Timing.** Fluid builds up over time. A scan in the first minutes after injury underestimates it.
- **Patient factors.** Obesity, subcutaneous emphysema and dressings all degrade the image.
- **Injury patterns.** Hollow viscus, diaphragm and retroperitoneal injuries produce little or no free fluid.

> **Pitfall** A negative FAST does not exclude intra-abdominal injury. Repeat the scan if the patient's condition changes, and use CT when the patient is stable enough to go.

?? You scan the right upper quadrant in a supine trauma patient. Where does a small volume of free fluid often appear first?
+ At the inferior tip of the liver, near the lower pole of the kidney
- Above the diaphragm
- In the gallbladder fossa
- Around the upper pole of the right kidney
: In the supine patient, fluid in the right upper quadrant tracks down to the caudal edge of the liver, so small volumes often show first at the liver tip. The space above the diaphragm holds haemothorax, not intraperitoneal fluid. The gallbladder fossa and the upper pole of the kidney sit higher and fill later.

?? Where do you place the probe for the right upper quadrant view?
+ Mid to posterior axillary line, 8th to 11th intercostal space, marker to the head
- Mid-clavicular line, 2nd intercostal space, marker to the head
- Subxiphoid, marker to the patient's right
- Right iliac fossa, marker to the umbilicus
: The right upper quadrant window lies on the flank, between the mid and posterior axillary lines, low on the ribs. It shows the liver, the right kidney and the space between them. The mid-clavicular line high on the chest is the pneumothorax window. The subxiphoid window shows the heart.

?? You see a black area between the liver and kidney. Which feature suggests free fluid rather than a structure?
+ Sharp, angled corners following the shape of the space
- A round outline with a smooth wall
- Colour flow within it
- A bright wall with posterior acoustic shadowing
: Free fluid fills the space it lies in, so it takes on angled corners and a stripe shape. A round outline with a smooth wall suggests a structure such as the gallbladder, a cyst or bowel. Colour flow means a vessel. A bright wall with shadowing describes bowel gas or a stone, not fluid.

?? A haemodynamically stable patient with abdominal trauma has a negative FAST. What is the best next step?
+ CT of the abdomen, with a repeat FAST if the patient's condition changes
- Discharge, as a negative FAST excludes significant injury
- Repeat the FAST in 24 hours before any further imaging
- Diagnostic laparotomy
: A negative FAST does not exclude injury, especially to hollow viscus, diaphragm and retroperitoneum. In a stable patient, CT gives the definitive assessment. Repeat the FAST if the condition changes. Discharge on a negative FAST misses injuries. Laparotomy is not indicated in a stable patient without other findings.

?? Which injury is a FAST scan most likely to miss?
+ An isolated bowel perforation
- A large splenic laceration with haemoperitoneum
- A large haemothorax
- A pericardial effusion with tamponade
: Bowel perforation releases little free fluid in the early hours, so the scan often looks normal. A large splenic bleed produces free fluid, which FAST detects well. A large haemothorax shows above the diaphragm, and a pericardial effusion shows in the cardiac view.
