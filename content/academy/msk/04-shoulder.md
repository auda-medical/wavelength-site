---
title: "The shoulder: one posterior view"
minutes: 10
---

One view from behind the patient shows whether the shoulder is dislocated, which way it has gone, and whether the reduction has worked, without moving the patient.

## The scan

1. Sit the patient up and stand behind them.
2. Use the curvilinear probe, or the linear probe in a slim patient.
3. Place the probe just below the scapular spine, parallel to it, and slide laterally until the back of the joint fills the screen.
4. Find the glenoid, a sharp bright edge on the medial side. Find the humeral head, a smooth round bright curve lateral to it. Check which side of the screen is medial before you judge anything.
5. Gently rotate the arm in and out, if pain allows, and watch the head turn in the joint.

## Reading the view

- **Normal:** the humeral head sits level with the glenoid rim and turns smoothly with rotation.
- **Anterior dislocation:** the head has moved forwards, so from behind it lies deeper, further from the probe than the glenoid.
- **Posterior dislocation:** the head has moved backwards, so it lies superficial, closer to the probe than the glenoid.
- **After reduction:** return to the same spot. The head sits level with the glenoid again and turns with rotation.

> **The aha** From behind, depth tells you direction. Head deep means anterior. Head superficial means posterior.

> **Pitfall** One still image misleads, because the angle of the probe changes how deep the head looks. Rotate the arm gently and compare with the other shoulder.

## What the scan does not tell you

Ultrasound shows position. It does not exclude a fracture of the greater tuberosity, the glenoid rim or the humeral neck. Follow your imaging policy, and X-ray when the mechanism, age or examination raises concern for a fracture. Check and document axillary nerve function before and after reduction.
