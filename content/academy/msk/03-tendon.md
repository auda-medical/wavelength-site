title: "Tendons: tear or anisotropy?"
minutes: 10

## The scan

1. Use the linear probe with plenty of gel.
2. Scan the tendon in long axis, then turn 90° to short axis.
3. Where the tendon curves, heel-toe the probe until the fibres brighten.
4. In short axis, tilt side to side.
5. Compare with the other side.
6. Move the joint gently and watch the tendon glide.

## Reading the view

- **Normal:** bright, parallel fibres in long axis, a bright speckled oval in short axis.
- **Anisotropy:** the tendon turns dark at an oblique angle and bright again when you correct it.
- **Partial tear:** a dark defect within the tendon which stays dark whatever the angle, with intact fibres beside it.
- **Full tear:** the fibres break across the whole tendon, often with a dark gap of fluid or blood. On movement, the ends do not glide together.

> **The aha** If the darkness disappears when you heel-toe, it was anisotropy.

> **Pitfall** Do not diagnose a tear from one dark longitudinal image. Correct the angle, check both planes and compare sides.

## Common emergency tendons

- **Achilles:** compare with the other side, and add the calf squeeze test while watching. A full tear shows a gap which widens with plantar flexion.
- **Hand extensors and flexors:** after lacerations, look for the cut ends and watch them on gentle finger movement. Hand injuries with a suspected tendon injury need hand surgery review.
- **Quadriceps and patellar tendons:** a gap above or below the patella after a fall onto a flexed knee.

## Use anisotropy to your advantage

A nerve and a tendon look alike in short axis. Tilt the probe. The tendon darkens with a small change in angle, the nerve changes little.
