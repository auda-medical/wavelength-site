A hot, swollen joint raises one urgent question: could this be septic arthritis? Ultrasound answers two narrower questions well. Is there fluid in the joint, and where is the safest place to aspirate it? It does not tell you whether the fluid is infected.

## The knee: the suprapatellar recess

The suprapatellar recess connects with the knee joint and fills first when fluid builds up. It lies deep to the quadriceps tendon, between the fat pads above the femur.

### The scan

1. Supine, knee flexed about 20 to 30° over a pillow. Slight flexion pushes fluid into the recess. Full flexion squeezes it out.
2. Linear probe in long axis just above the patella.
3. Find the quadriceps tendon, the top of the patella and the femur.
4. Look between the tendon and the femur for the recess. Turn to transverse and sweep medially and laterally.
5. If the pocket is small, press gently on either side of the patella to push fluid up under the probe.

### Findings

- **Normal:** a collapsed recess, a thin dark line between the fat pads.
- **Effusion:** a black or grey space deep to the tendon, compressible and moving with pressure.
- **Complex fluid:** debris, strands or a fluid level. Infection, blood and crystal arthritis all cause this.
- **Synovial thickening:** grey tissue that neither compresses nor moves.
- **Prepatellar bursitis:** fluid in front of the patella, outside the joint. Do not mistake it for an effusion.

## The shoulder: the posterior recess

Glenohumeral fluid shows most reliably in the posterior recess, especially with the arm externally rotated.

### The scan

1. Sit the patient up and stand behind them, forearm resting on the lap.
2. Linear probe, or curvilinear in a larger patient, just below the scapular spine and parallel to it. Slide laterally to the back of the joint.
3. Find the glenoid, the humeral head lateral to it, and the posterior labrum between them.
4. Rotate the arm outwards with the elbow at the side, and watch the recess fill.
5. Compare with the other shoulder using the same settings.

### Findings

- **Normal:** the capsule lies close to the humeral head, with at most a thin dark line, usually 2 mm or less.
- **Effusion:** a dark space lifts the capsule off the humeral head and bulges outwards.
- **Asymmetry:** a recess clearly wider than the other side supports a true effusion.

## The elbow: the posterior recess

With the elbow bent to about 90°, put the linear probe in long axis over the back of the elbow, above the olecranon. Find the triceps tendon, the posterior fat pad and the dip of the olecranon fossa.

- **Normal:** the fat pad sits down in the fossa, with no visible fluid.
- **Effusion:** dark fluid in the posterior recess lifts the fat pad out of the fossa, the same sign as the raised posterior fat pad on a lateral X-ray.
- **After trauma:** an effusion suggests an injury inside the joint, but ultrasound may not show the fracture line. Treat it as a possible occult fracture and follow your fracture pathway.

### Joint or bursa?

The olecranon bursa sits on top of the olecranon and the triceps insertion, just under the skin. Fluid there is a bursitis, not an effusion. Scan it with almost no pressure, or the bursa empties. A complex bursa with debris and hyperaemia makes infection more likely, but only aspiration tells you. ## The ankle: the anterior recess

Put the linear probe in long axis over the front of the ankle, in the midline. Find the tibia and the dome of the talus. Joint fluid sits in the anterior recess between them, deep to the extensor tendons. Gentle plantarflexion pushes fluid forward and makes a small effusion easier to see. Fluid around a tendon is tenosynovitis, not a joint effusion. When you aspirate from the front, find the dorsalis pedis artery with colour Doppler first. The Wavelength Pearl [Ankle effusion: open the anterior recess](/learn/ankle-effusion-ultrasound/) shows each step.

> **The aha** Find the pocket. If the recess opens, believe the fluid.

> **Pitfall** Ultrasound finds fluid. It does not separate septic from inflammatory or crystal arthritis, and a small or absent effusion does not exclude infection. A joint that looks infected needs aspiration and specialist review.

## The wrist: the dorsal recess

Put the linear probe in long axis over the back of the wrist, in line with the middle finger. Joint fluid lifts the dorsal recess over the lunate and capitate, deep to the extensor tendons. Fluid that moves with a tendon is tenosynovitis; a well-defined cyst with a neck is a ganglion. After a fall, an effusion may mean a fracture, including the scaphoid. ## Guided aspiration

1. Choose the largest, most superficial pocket away from vessels: superolateral for the knee, posterior for the shoulder and the elbow, anterior for the ankle.
2. Mark or scan in real time. Use full aseptic technique and a sterile probe cover.
3. Insert the needle in plane, so you see its whole length, and watch the tip enter the fluid. For the shoulder, go from lateral to medial towards the space between the humeral head and the glenoid.
4. Send fluid for microscopy, Gram stain, culture and crystals before antibiotics when the patient is stable.

## Case

A 68-year-old on immunosuppression has a hot, swollen right knee and a temperature of 38.2°C. Ultrasound shows a large complex effusion in the suprapatellar recess, largest superolaterally.

?? What is the next step?
+ Ultrasound-guided aspiration from the superolateral recess, fluid for culture and crystals, then urgent orthopaedic review
- Diagnose gout from the scan and start colchicine
- Start antibiotics and discharge without aspiration
- Repeat the scan in a week
: Complex fluid has many causes. Fever and immunosuppression make septic arthritis a real risk. Aspirate under guidance, send fluid before antibiotics if the patient is stable, and involve orthopaedics urgently.
