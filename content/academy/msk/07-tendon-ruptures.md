Three tendon ruptures are often missed at first presentation, because patients keep some movement: the Achilles, the distal biceps at the elbow, and the quadriceps above the knee. In all three, the scan follows the tendon all the way to its insertion and adds movement when a still image is unclear.

## The Achilles tendon

Patients describe a kick or a blow to the back of the heel, often during a sprint or a jump. They can still walk and plantarflex using tibialis posterior, the peroneal muscles and the long toe flexors. The calf squeeze test is the key examination: squeezing the calf of a prone patient normally plantarflexes the foot.

### The scan

1. Prone, feet hanging over the end of the bed.
2. Linear probe, long axis, from the calcaneal insertion up to the calf muscles.
3. Repeat in short axis.
4. Squeeze the calf, or gently move the ankle, while you watch the tendon in long axis.
5. Compare with the other side.

### Findings

- **Normal:** a continuous, bright, fibrillar band which slides as one when you squeeze the calf.
- **Complete rupture:** a gap filled with dark fluid or mixed haematoma, most often a few centimetres above the insertion, with frayed ends. Fat from behind the tendon can bulge into the gap. Movement does not pass across the gap.
- **Partial tear or tendinopathy:** a thick, dark tendon with some continuous fibres. Movement passes through.
- **Xanthoma:** smooth thickening with a speckled texture and continuous fibres, often painless and on both sides. It suggests familial hypercholesterolaemia: check lipids. See the Wavelength Pearl [Achilles lump: xanthoma or tear?](/learn/achilles-xanthoma-ultrasound/)

> **Pitfall** The thin plantaris tendon often survives an Achilles rupture and runs along the medial side. Do not count it as intact Achilles fibres.

## The distal biceps tendon

Distal biceps tears usually happen in middle-aged men lifting a heavy load with the elbow bent. Patients describe a pop, then bruising and weakness of supination. In the hook test, the examiner cannot hook a finger under the tendon from the lateral side. Repair works best within the first few weeks, before the tendon retracts and scars, so refer early.

An intact bicipital aponeurosis, the lacertus fibrosus, can hold a torn tendon near the elbow crease. The muscle bulge may barely move, and a scan which stops at the crease looks normal.

### The scan

1. Elbow extended, forearm supinated.
2. Linear probe, long axis, over the front of the elbow. Follow the tendon down from above the crease.
3. The tendon dives deep towards the radius. Press the lower end of the probe harder to keep the beam at 90° to the fibres.
4. Follow it to the radial tuberosity, then confirm in short axis.
5. Rotate the forearm slowly from supination to pronation while you watch the insertion.
6. Compare with the other arm.

### Findings

- **Normal:** continuous fibres reach the radial tuberosity and move with it on rotation.
- **Complete tear:** an empty tuberosity, a wavy retracted stump higher up, fluid or haematoma in the tendon path.
- **Partial tear:** a thick or thin tendon with dark areas, some fibres still reaching the bone.

> **The aha** Trace to the insertion, not to the elbow crease. The empty destination makes the diagnosis.

## The quadriceps tendon

Patients are usually over 40 and describe a stumble or a fall onto a bent knee. Swelling hides the defect, the X-ray looks near normal, and some patients can still lift the leg a little using the retinacula at the sides of the patella. Inability to perform a full straight-leg raise against gravity is the key examination finding.

### The scan

1. Supine, knee straight or slightly bent over a pillow.
2. Linear probe, long axis, above the patella, marker to the head.
3. Follow the bright, fibrillar tendon down to its insertion on the top of the patella.
4. Sweep medial to lateral across the whole width, then confirm in short axis.
5. Gently bend the knee a little, if pain allows, and watch the insertion.
6. Compare with the other knee. Look at the patellar tendon below the patella too.

### Findings

- **Normal:** a continuous band of parallel fibres reaching the superior pole of the patella.
- **Complete rupture:** a gap, usually within 2 cm of the patella, filled with fluid or haematoma, with wavy retracted ends. The gap widens as the knee bends.
- **Partial tear:** loss of some fibres, with part of the tendon still continuous through the full length.
- **Anisotropy:** a dark area that lights up when you rock the probe heel to toe.

> **The aha** Trust the gap more than the blur. Gentle flexion opens a true full-thickness tear.

## The patellar tendon

The same extensor mechanism continues below the patella. Patellar tendon ruptures are less common, and tend to affect younger patients after a forceful contraction, or people with tendinopathy, steroid use or systemic disease. The patient cannot straight-leg raise, and the patella may sit higher than on the other side.

1. Knee slightly bent over a pillow, linear probe in long axis below the patella.
2. Follow the tendon from the lower pole of the patella all the way to the tibial tubercle. Tears happen at either end and in the middle.
3. Turn to short axis at any abnormal spot, and compare with the other knee.

A complete rupture shows a dark gap of fluid or haematoma with wavy retracted ends. Heel-toe the probe before you call a dark patch a tear. The Wavelength Pearl [Patellar tendon rupture: follow it to the tibial tubercle](/learn/patellar-tendon-rupture-ultrasound/) shows each step.

## What to do with the finding

- **Complete Achilles rupture:** immobilise in plantarflexion and refer through your trust's Achilles pathway the same day.
- **Distal biceps rupture:** refer to the upper limb or orthopaedic team urgently for an early repair decision.
- **Complete quadriceps or patellar tendon rupture:** splint the knee straight and refer urgently to orthopaedics, as early repair gives the best results.
- **Unclear scan with a convincing story:** refer for specialist review and imaging. Do not reassure on a scan you could not complete.

## Case

A 46-year-old man felt a pop in his elbow lifting a wardrobe. There is bruising in the forearm. The tendon looks normal in the elbow crease. Following it down, the radial tuberosity is bare, with fluid around it and a retracted stump 3 cm above.

?? What is the diagnosis and the next step?
+ A complete distal biceps rupture held near the crease by the lacertus fibrosus, referred urgently for repair
- A biceps strain, treated with rest and a sling
- A partial tear, reviewed in clinic in six weeks
- A normal tendon, as it looks intact in the elbow crease
: The bare tuberosity and the retracted stump confirm a complete tear. An intact lacertus fibrosus limits retraction, so the tendon looks normal higher up. Early referral protects the chance of a straightforward repair.
