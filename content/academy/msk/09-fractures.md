Ultrasound cannot see through adult bone, but it shows the bone surface very well. A fracture breaks that surface. The skill is to trace the cortex, find the break and confirm it in a second plane. Distal forearm fractures in children are the best-studied use. The same method works on any bone surface you can reach, such as the ribs, the clavicle, the metacarpals and the nasal bones.

## The scan

1. Use the linear probe and plenty of gel. Start away from the most painful point, on normal bone.
2. Find the cortex: a bright, smooth, continuous line with a black shadow beneath it.
3. Slide slowly along it in long axis towards the tender area.
4. At any step, gap, buckle or angle, turn the probe 90° and find the same break in short axis.
5. Look beside the break for haematoma and a lifted periosteum, and check that the tenderness is under the probe.
6. Compare with the other side when the anatomy is complex, especially in children.

## Findings

- **Normal cortex:** bright, smooth and unbroken.
- **Fracture:** a break, step or angle in the cortex, seen in two planes. In a child's buckle fracture, the cortex bulges or kinks rather than breaking cleanly.
- **Supporting signs:** haematoma beside the bone, a lifted periosteum, soft tissue swelling and focal tenderness. They support a fracture but do not prove one.
- **Mimics:** growth plates, ossification centres and nutrient vessels in children, and old healed injuries in adults.

> **The aha** Follow the cortex, not the shadow. The break is in the bright line at the top.

> **Pitfall** A normal scan of one surface does not exclude a fracture elsewhere in the bone, or one into a joint. If you cannot see the whole relevant surface, or the patient's story and examination still worry you, get an X-ray.

## What to do with the finding

- A clear fracture on ultrasound still needs management through your fracture pathway: immobilisation, an X-ray where your pathway requires one to define displacement or joint involvement, and follow-up.
- A normal scan helps only when it answers a focused question over a surface you could fully see. It does not replace an X-ray in a patient you would otherwise X-ray.
- An effusion after trauma, as in the elbow, means a possible fracture inside the joint even when no fracture line is seen.

## Ribs and the clavicle

**Ribs.** Scan along the rib at the point of tenderness. Ultrasound finds many fractures an X-ray misses, including breaks in the costal cartilage. Then look under the rib for lung sliding and fluid: a pneumothorax or haemothorax matters more than the crack. Older patients and those with several fractures need your local chest injury pathway. See [Rib fracture: trace the cortex](/learn/rib-fracture-ultrasound/).

**Clavicle.** The bone is superficial from the sternum to the acromion, so its cortex is easy to follow, which is useful in children. Keep the probe on the line of the bone as it curves at the ends. Tented skin and a posterior sternoclavicular dislocation need urgent orthopaedic review. See [Clavicle: follow the bright line](/learn/clavicle-fracture-ultrasound/).

## Case

An 8-year-old fell onto an outstretched hand and has tenderness over the dorsal distal radius, with no deformity. In long axis over the distal radius, the cortex bulges and kinks about 3 cm from the growth plate. You find the same kink in short axis. The other wrist is smooth.

?? What does this show, and what next?
+ A buckle fracture of the distal radius, seen in two planes. Manage it through the paediatric fracture pathway
- A normal growth plate
- A ligament sprain, needing no follow-up
- An effusion of the wrist joint
: A cortical bulge seen in two planes and absent on the other side is a buckle fracture. The growth plate is a separate, smooth gap at the end of the bone. Follow your local pathway, which may include an X-ray and a splint.
