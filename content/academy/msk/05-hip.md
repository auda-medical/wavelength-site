title: "The hip: compare the capsule"
minutes: 12
---

A limping child or an adult with a painful, hot hip raises the question of a joint effusion. Ultrasound shows fluid in the hip quickly and guides aspiration. It does not tell you what the fluid is.

## The scan

1. Lie the patient supine with the hip in neutral, or slightly flexed and externally rotated if that is more comfortable.
2. Use the linear probe in children and slim adults, the curvilinear probe in larger patients.
3. Place the probe just below the inguinal ligament, lateral to the femoral vessels, aligned along the long axis of the femoral neck, marker towards the head.
4. Find the femoral head, a bright curve, and the femoral neck running from it. The anterior capsule lies as a bright line over the neck.
5. Measure the distance from the front of the femoral neck to the outer edge of the capsule, at the widest point.
6. Repeat on the other side.

## Reading the view

- **Normal:** the capsule lies close to the neck and follows its concave curve.
- **Effusion:** a dark space lifts the capsule off the neck, and the capsule bulges outwards.
- **In children,** ACEP guidance defines an effusion as a capsule-to-neck distance over 5 mm, or a difference of more than 2 mm compared with the other hip.

> **The aha** When unsure, scan the other hip. Asymmetry is the strongest sign.

> **Pitfall** An effusion does not separate transient synovitis from septic arthritis. Use the clinical picture: fever, refusal to bear weight, raised white cell count and raised inflammatory markers, the factors described by Kocher and colleagues. Suspected septic arthritis needs urgent orthopaedic review and joint aspiration.

## Guided aspiration

Ultrasound shows the anterior recess as the target, and the femoral vessels medial to it. Use colour Doppler to locate the vessels, and an in-plane approach from lateral to medial. Aspiration of a septic hip is usually an orthopaedic procedure. Follow your local pathway.
