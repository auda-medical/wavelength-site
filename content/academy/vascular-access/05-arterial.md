---
title: "Arterial lines: the radial artery in a shocked patient"
minutes: 12
---

In a shocked patient the radial pulse is weak and the artery is small, so palpation misleads. Ultrasound shows the artery, its walls and the structures beside it.

## The evidence

A 2014 systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials found that ultrasound guidance improves first-attempt success and reduces the number of attempts for radial artery catheterisation.

## Before you start

Record consent where possible, check the hand is well perfused, and choose the limb according to your trust's policy. Use sterile technique and local anaesthetic in the awake patient.

## The scan

1. **Position.** Support the wrist on a roll or folded towel in gentle extension, and keep the hand still. Over-extension can flatten the artery, so stop short of the end of range.
2. **Identify.** In short axis over the distal forearm, find the round, pulsating, non-compressible artery. It lies close to a tendon and usually has a vein on each side. The tendon is bright, fibrillar and does not pulsate.
3. **Choose.** Slide a few centimetres up and down the forearm. Pick a straight, superficial segment with a clear lumen and a stable wall. Avoid bifurcations, branches and a mobile wrist segment.
4. **Enter.** Use a low angle. It gives better control of the tip and an easier catheter entry.
5. **Advance.** When you see pulsatile blood return, lower the angle, advance and thread the catheter under vision.

## The rescue

If the puncture succeeds but the catheter will not advance, rotate to long axis and look at the tip and the wall, as for a peripheral vein. Do not push blindly.

## Afterwards

After a removed line or a failed attempt, apply firm pressure until bleeding stops. Check the hand's colour and warmth, and watch for haematoma.

> **The aha** Scan a few centimetres before you choose. The strongest pulse is not always the easiest segment.
