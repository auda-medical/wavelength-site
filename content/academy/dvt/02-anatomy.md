You need four landmarks: the common femoral vein, the saphenofemoral junction, the femoral vein bifurcation and the popliteal vein. Each sits next to an artery, and the artery is your guide.

![Wavelength Pearl: DVT scan. The common femoral vein lies medial to the common femoral artery at the groin.](/learn/images/dvt-pearl.webp)

## The groin

Just below the inguinal ligament, the order from lateral to medial is nerve, artery, vein. The **common femoral vein (CFV)** lies medial to the **common femoral artery (CFA)**. In transverse, the vein is the larger, oval structure. The artery is round, thick-walled and pulsatile.

## The saphenofemoral junction

A few centimetres below the inguinal ligament, the **great saphenous vein** joins the CFV from the medial side. In transverse, the CFV with the artery on one side and the saphenous vein on the other gives the "Mickey Mouse" sign: the CFV is the head, the artery and the saphenous vein are the ears.

The great saphenous vein is a superficial vein. Clot in it is superficial vein thrombosis, not DVT. Clot near the junction is close to the deep system, so it needs formal assessment.

## The bifurcation

A little further down, the CFV divides into two deep veins:

- The **femoral vein**, which runs down the thigh with the femoral artery.
- The **deep femoral vein**, which dives deep and laterally into the thigh muscles.

The artery divides higher than the vein, so you often see two arteries beside one vein before the vein splits.

> **The aha** The femoral vein used to be called the "superficial femoral vein". It is a deep vein. Clot in it is a DVT. Some reports still use the old name.

## The thigh

The femoral vein runs with the artery under the sartorius muscle, through the adductor canal. In the lower thigh it lies deep and becomes hard to compress. It passes through the adductor hiatus to the back of the knee, where it becomes the popliteal vein.

## The popliteal fossa

Scanning from behind, the order flips. The **popliteal vein** lies between the skin and the **popliteal artery**, so it is the first vessel you meet. It runs down to the upper calf, where it divides into the calf veins (the trifurcation).

## Variants

Duplication of the femoral and popliteal veins is common. A duplicated vein looks like two veins beside one artery. Compress both. A clot in one of a pair is still a DVT.
