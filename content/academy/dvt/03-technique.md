---
title: "Technique: the two-region compression scan"
minutes: 10
---

The scan rests on one fact. A normal vein collapses completely under gentle pressure. A vein containing clot does not. You do not need to see the clot. You need to see whether the walls meet.

## Set up

1. Explain the scan and get consent. Offer a chaperone for the groin.
2. Raise the head of the bed 10° to 30°. This fills the leg veins and makes them easier to see and compress.
3. Turn the hip out and bend the knee slightly, the "frog leg" position.
4. Choose the linear probe and the venous or vascular preset. In a large or oedematous leg, switch to the curvilinear probe.
5. Start with a depth of 4 to 6 cm. Set the focus at the level of the vein.
6. Hold the probe transverse across the leg, with the marker to the patient's right.

## Region one: the groin

1. Find the CFV and CFA just below the inguinal ligament.
2. Start just above the saphenofemoral junction.
3. Press straight down until the vein walls meet. Release.
4. Slide 1 cm down the leg. Press again.
5. Keep going through the saphenofemoral junction and past the bifurcation into the femoral and deep femoral veins.

## Region two: the popliteal fossa

1. Roll the patient onto their side, sit them with the leg hanging, or keep them supine with the knee bent and the probe behind it.
2. Find the popliteal vein, closest to the probe, with the artery deep to it.
3. Compress from the popliteal crease down to where the vein divides in the upper calf, every 1 cm.

## Two-point, three-point or two-region?

Protocols differ. Two-point scans compress at the CFV and the popliteal vein. Three-point scans add the femoral vein in the thigh. The two-region scan, described here, compresses continuously through each region, so a short clot between two points is not missed. The 2024 systematic review found three-point scans more sensitive than two-point. Follow your local protocol, and compress more points, not fewer.

## How hard to press

Press until the artery starts to deform. If the artery dents and the vein walls meet, the segment is normal. If the artery dents and the vein stays open, even partly, the segment is abnormal.

> **The aha** The artery is your pressure gauge. Too little pressure makes every vein look abnormal.

> **Pitfall** Compress in transverse only. In longitudinal, the probe slides off the side of the vein, the vein leaves the screen, and it looks as though it has collapsed.

## Doppler: when it helps

Compression answers the question. Colour Doppler helps you find a vessel or confirm flow. Spectral Doppler in the CFV adds one clue: normal flow varies with breathing. A flat, continuous signal without respiratory variation suggests a blockage higher up, in the iliac veins or the IVC, which the compression scan does not reach.
