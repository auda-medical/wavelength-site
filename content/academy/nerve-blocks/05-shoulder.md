Anterior shoulder dislocation is one of the commonest reasons for procedural sedation in the emergency department. A block lets the patient stay awake while you reduce the shoulder, and frees the sedation team and the resuscitation bay.

## The phrenic problem

The shoulder joint takes its nerve supply mainly from the C5 and C6 roots, through the suprascapular and axillary nerves and small capsular branches. The classic block at this level, the interscalene block, works well, but the phrenic nerve runs close to the C5 root on the front of anterior scalene. An interscalene block paralyses the diaphragm on that side in most patients. Two newer blocks move the injection away from the phrenic nerve.

## The superior trunk block

The C5 and C6 roots join to form the superior trunk just above the clavicle. Injecting here, below the point where the phrenic nerve lies closest, reduces diaphragm involvement.

### The evidence

- A randomised trial in shoulder surgery found the superior trunk block gave analgesia comparable to an interscalene block, with less diaphragm paralysis.
- A 2026 meta-analysis of eight surgical trials found the superior trunk block reduced complete hemidiaphragmatic paralysis by about 90% compared with the interscalene block.
- A German emergency department reviewed 124 shoulder reductions under low-volume superior trunk blocks of about 4 to 5 mL over six years. Every reduction succeeded, and no clinically relevant respiratory or haemodynamic complications were reported. Diaphragm function was not measured.

### The technique

1. Sitting up or supine with the head turned away.
2. Linear probe transverse at the level of the cricoid. Find C5, C6 and C7 stacked between anterior and middle scalene.
3. Follow C5 and C6 towards the clavicle until they merge into one oval, before the suprascapular nerve leaves it.
4. In-plane needle from the lateral side, through middle scalene. The dorsal scapular and long thoracic nerves run in middle scalene, so look for them and plan the path around them.
5. Inject in small aliquots beside the trunk, watching the spread.

### Who should not have it

- Severe respiratory disease, where losing one hemidiaphragm could cause respiratory failure.
- A known paralysed diaphragm on the other side.
- A patient who has already had a block on the other side of the neck.

## The SHAC block

The shoulder anterior capsular block was described in 2021. It places local anaesthetic on the front of the shoulder joint, between subscapularis and the anterior capsule, and in the plane between deltoid and subscapularis. It works far from the neck, so it spares the brachial plexus roots and the phrenic nerve.

### The evidence

The first report used 10 to 15 mL of 0.2% ropivacaine with dexamethasone in patients with painful shoulders. Pain scores fell by around 6 points out of 10, with no motor block and no adverse events. Evidence for dislocation and fractures of the upper humerus in the emergency department is limited to small reports.

### The technique

1. Supine, arm by the side, neutral or slight external rotation if comfortable.
2. Linear probe transverse on the front of the shoulder, just below and lateral to the coracoid.
3. Identify the coracoid, the humeral head, the subscapularis tendon over it and the deltoid above.
4. In-plane needle, keeping the whole shaft in view, aimed deep to subscapularis onto the capsule over the humeral head, lateral to the coracoid.
5. Watch fluid spread along the capsule, under the tendon.

> **Pitfall** The axillary artery, the axillary vein and the brachial plexus run medial to the coracoid. Keep the needle lateral to it. A swelling tendon and high pressure mean the tip is inside the tendon.

## Choosing

- **Superior trunk block:** the stronger evidence for complete shoulder analgesia. Avoid it in patients who cannot tolerate losing a hemidiaphragm.
- **SHAC block:** no phrenic risk, less evidence in emergency care. Consider it when the diaphragm matters most, within local governance.
- **Sedation:** still the right choice for some patients, and the back-up when a block fails.

> **The aha** Move the needle away from the neck and you move the risk away from the diaphragm.
