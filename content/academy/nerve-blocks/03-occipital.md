The greater occipital nerve arises from the C2 root, curves up through the back of the neck and pierces the muscles to reach the scalp at the level of the superior nuchal line. There, it runs beside the occipital artery, usually on its medial side. Blocking it can relieve migraine and occipital headache, especially with tenderness over the nerve.

The Wavelength Pearl [Occipital nerve block: follow the artery, then find the nerve](/learn/occipital-nerve-block-follow-the-artery/) summarises the technique.

## The evidence

In a randomised, sham-controlled emergency department trial, patients with migraine which persisted after intravenous metoclopramide received bilateral blocks with 0.5% bupivacaine or a sham scalp injection. At 30 minutes, 4 of 13 patients after the block were headache-free, compared with none of 15 after the sham. Slow recruitment stopped the trial early, so the result is promising rather than definitive.

## Exclude the dangerous headache first

A block relieves pain whatever its cause. Before you block, take a full headache history and examine the patient. Thunderclap onset, focal neurology, fever, neck stiffness, papilloedema, immunosuppression, pregnancy or the postpartum period, or a new headache over the age of 50 needs investigation first.

## Two levels

- **Distal, at the superior nuchal line.** The nerve lies beside the occipital artery. Colour Doppler finds the artery quickly. This is the level shown in the Pearl.
- **Proximal, at C2.** The nerve runs on the superficial surface of obliquus capitis inferior, deep to semispinalis capitis. Here it is larger and lies on a constant muscle layer. A study comparing the two levels confirmed both with anatomical dissection. Experienced operators often prefer the proximal level.

## The distal technique

1. Sit the patient forward with the neck slightly flexed, or lie them prone.
2. Linear probe transverse at the superior nuchal line, about a third of the way from the occipital protuberance to the mastoid.
3. Colour Doppler: find the occipital artery.
4. Find the nerve beside it: a small dark oval with a bright rim and no flow.
5. In-plane needle, aspirate, inject in small aliquots and watch fluid surround the nerve.
6. Repeat on the other side for bilateral symptoms.

## Risks

- Intravascular injection into the occipital artery.
- Bleeding and bruising, especially on anticoagulants.
- Light-headedness after injection.
- Masking a secondary headache, which is why the assessment comes first.

> **Pitfall** A block treats the pain, not the diagnosis. Safety-net every patient and arrange follow-up for recurrent headache.
