---
title: "Why blocks belong in the emergency department"
minutes: 10
---

Pain is the commonest reason people come to the emergency department, and opioids and sedation carry their own risks. An ultrasound-guided block treats pain where it starts. It can spare an older patient with rib fractures from opioid drowsiness, or reduce a shoulder without a sedation bay.

This module covers four blocks:

- **Greater occipital nerve block** for migraine and occipital headache.
- **Serratus anterior plane block** for rib fractures and painful chest drains.
- **Superior trunk block** for shoulder dislocation and fractures of the upper humerus.
- **Shoulder anterior capsular (SHAC) block**, an emerging option for the shoulder which spares the phrenic nerve.

## What this module is, and is not

This module teaches the theory: the safety system, the anatomy on the screen, the needle path and the evidence. It does not make you competent to perform a block. Hands-on skill comes from supervised practice on patients, under your trust's governance.

> **Before your first block** Check your trust's approved list of emergency department blocks, its training requirements and its sign-off process. Do your first blocks with a supervisor at your side.

## Who should not have a block

- A patient who declines, or who cannot consent and does not need a block in their best interests.
- Infection at the injection site.
- Allergy to local anaesthetic.
- A block which would mask an evolving problem, such as compartment syndrome, without a plan to monitor for it.
- Block-specific reasons, covered in each lesson. For example, a superior trunk block in a patient whose other diaphragm does not work.

Anticoagulants are a relative contraindication for superficial blocks in compressible sites. Follow your trust's guidance and weigh the benefit.

## The evidence in brief

- **Serratus anterior plane block:** the SABRE trial in eight Australian emergency departments showed more patients with a meaningful fall in pain at 4 hours, and half the opioid use over 24 hours.
- **Greater occipital nerve block:** a small emergency department trial, stopped early, showed more patients headache-free at 30 minutes than after a sham injection.
- **Superior trunk block:** strong surgical evidence that it spares the diaphragm compared with an interscalene block, and an emergency department series of 124 shoulder reductions with low volumes.
- **SHAC block:** described in 2021, with small series so far. Treat it as an advanced, emerging option.
