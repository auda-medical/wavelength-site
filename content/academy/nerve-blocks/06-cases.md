Five patients from the emergency department. Read each case, choose your answer, then read the explanation. These cases do not count towards your score.

## Case 1: the older faller

An 82-year-old woman fell at home and has four fractured ribs on the right side, in the mid-axillary line. She cannot take a deep breath, and morphine has made her drowsy. Ultrasound shows normal lung sliding on the right.

?? Which block suits her best?
+ A serratus anterior plane block on the right
- A superior trunk block on the right
- A greater occipital nerve block
- A SHAC block on the right
: Lateral rib fractures lie in the territory of the serratus anterior plane block. In the SABRE trial, the block increased the proportion of patients with a meaningful fall in pain and halved opioid use.

## Case 2: the tingling lips

Five minutes after a serratus anterior plane block with a large volume of levobupivacaine, the patient says her lips are tingling and she has a metallic taste. She becomes agitated.

?? What is happening, and what do you do first?
+ Early local anaesthetic toxicity: stop injecting, call for help and give oxygen
- Anxiety: reassure her and carry on
- An allergic reaction: give adrenaline intramuscularly and continue the block
- Normal block onset: wait for it to settle
: Tingling around the mouth, a metallic taste and agitation are early signs of toxicity. Stop, call for help, manage the airway with oxygen and prepare lipid emulsion. Seizures and cardiovascular collapse can follow quickly.

## Case 3: the thunderclap

A 44-year-old man has a severe occipital headache which reached its peak within a minute while lifting weights. He has a history of migraine and asks for the nerve block which helped him last year.

?? What do you do?
+ Investigate for subarachnoid haemorrhage before considering a block
- Perform a bilateral greater occipital nerve block
- Give a triptan and discharge
- Perform the block and arrange a CT scan if it fails
: A thunderclap onset needs investigation for subarachnoid haemorrhage, whatever the past history. A block could relieve the pain and hide a life-threatening diagnosis.

## Case 4: the breathless dislocation

A 70-year-old man with severe COPD, on home oxygen, has a first anterior dislocation of the left shoulder. His sedation risk is high.

?? Which approach suits him best?
+ A SHAC block, which spares the phrenic nerve, within local governance
- A high-volume interscalene block
- A superior trunk block, as it never affects the diaphragm
- A greater occipital nerve block
: An interscalene block paralyses the diaphragm on that side in most patients. A superior trunk block reduces this risk but does not remove it. In a patient who could not tolerate losing a hemidiaphragm, a SHAC block avoids the phrenic nerve, though its emergency evidence is limited.

## Case 5: the wrong side

You have scanned the right side of the chest and prepared the skin. A colleague interrupts to ask about another patient. You return with the needle in your hand.

?? What do you do before the needle touches the skin?
+ Stop and confirm the side against the site marking and with the patient
- Proceed, as you scanned the correct side earlier
- Ask the nurse to confirm the drug only
- Re-prepare the skin and proceed
: Wrong-side blocks often follow a distraction between the checks and the needle. Stop Before You Block adds a final check of the side immediately before needle insertion.
