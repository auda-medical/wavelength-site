title: "Serratus anterior plane block for rib fractures"
minutes: 15

Rib fractures hurt with every breath. Patients who cannot breathe deeply or cough develop atelectasis and pneumonia, and older patients do worse. Good analgesia is part of treatment, not a comfort measure.

The serratus anterior plane block was first described in 2013. Local anaesthetic spread in the plane around serratus anterior reaches the lateral cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves, numbing the side of the chest wall.

The Wavelength Pearl [Serratus plane block: see the rib, see the pleura, then open the plane](/learn/serratus-plane-block-see-the-rib/) summarises the technique.

## The evidence

The SABRE trial randomised 210 adults with rib fractures in eight Australian emergency departments to a serratus anterior plane block plus standard care, or standard care alone. At 4 hours, 41% of the block group had a meaningful fall in pain, compared with 20% of the control group. Median opioid use over 24 hours fell from 91 to 45 mg morphine equivalents.

## Coverage

- **Good:** fractures on the side of the chest wall, from about the second to the ninth rib.
- **Less reliable:** posterior fractures near the spine, which may need a different block, such as an erector spinae plane block.

## The technique

1. Supine or lying on the uninjured side, arm moved away from the chest.
2. Linear probe in the mid-axillary line over the fourth or fifth rib, sagittal.
3. Identify the layers: latissimus dorsi at the back, serratus anterior lying on the ribs, the rib with its shadow, the pleura sliding between the ribs.
4. In-plane needle aimed at the rib.
5. Deep approach: the tip rests between serratus anterior and the rib. Superficial approach: between latissimus dorsi and serratus anterior.
6. Hydrodissect with 0.5 to 1 mL, confirm the plane opens, then inject in aliquots.

Plane blocks rely on volume to spread. That volume makes the total dose the main safety concern. Calculate it first, and keep within the maximum for the patient's weight.

> **The aha** Aim for the rib. Bone under the tip keeps the needle away from the pleura.

## Risks

- Pneumothorax, if the needle passes between the ribs.
- Local anaesthetic toxicity, because of the volume.
- Intramuscular injection and a failed block, if the tip sits in the muscle.

Before the block, check for a pneumothorax with ultrasound or X-ray, so that you know the baseline. After the block, look again if the patient becomes more breathless.

> **Pitfall** Never lose sight of the tip or the pleura. If you cannot see both, stop and rescan.
