## The appendix

Ultrasound is the first-line image for suspected appendicitis in children and in pregnancy, and avoids radiation. A 2017 meta-analysis by Fields and colleagues found emergency physician ultrasound to be highly specific for appendicitis, with lower and more variable sensitivity. A positive scan helps. A negative scan does not close the case.

1. Use the linear probe, or the curvilinear probe in larger patients.
2. Start where the patient points to the pain. Find the caecum and terminal ileum.
3. Use graded compression: slow, steady pressure which pushes gas aside.
4. Look for a blind-ending tube arising from the caecum which does not compress.
5. Measure its outer diameter in transverse. Confirm in long axis.

**Supports appendicitis:** a blind-ending, non-compressible tube over 6 mm across, bright inflamed fat around it, increased flow on colour Doppler, an appendicolith (a bright focus with a shadow), or a collection.

**Normal appendix:** compressible, thin-walled, under 6 mm.

> **Pitfall** Not seeing the appendix is a non-diagnostic scan, not a negative one. Follow your local pathway: scoring systems, formal ultrasound, MRI in pregnancy or CT in adults.

## Intussusception in children

Intussusception is the commonest cause of bowel obstruction between about three months and three years: intermittent colicky pain, drawing up the legs, periods of calm, sometimes vomiting or blood in the stool. A 2020 meta-analysis found emergency physician ultrasound had a sensitivity of 94.9% and a specificity of 99.1% for ileocolic intussusception.

1. Use the linear probe. Follow the colon from the right lower quadrant to the right upper quadrant, across and down the left.
2. In transverse, look for the **target sign:** alternating bright and dark rings of bowel inside bowel, usually 2.5 cm or more across.
3. In long axis, confirm the **sandwich or pseudokidney sign.**
4. Look for trapped mesenteric fat and vessels, and for a lead point.

> **The aha** See it in two planes before you call it.

> **Pitfall** Transient small-bowel intussusceptions are smaller, central or left-sided, lack a lead point and usually reduce on their own. Rescan and use the clinical picture.

A positive scan needs urgent paediatric surgical and radiology referral. A negative bedside scan in a convincing story needs formal imaging.
