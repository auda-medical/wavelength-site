Obstructed small bowel fills with fluid, and fluid carries ultrasound well. The dilated loops show clearly, often before an X-ray shows the classic pattern.

## The scan

1. Use the curvilinear probe.
2. Sweep the whole abdomen in rows, top to bottom, first in transverse and then in long axis.
3. Measure the widest small bowel loop, outer wall to outer wall.
4. Watch the contents of a dilated loop for 10 to 20 seconds.
5. Follow the dilated loops until they become collapsed.

## What to look for

- **Dilated loops:** small bowel over 2.5 cm across. Fluid-filled loops with thin folds crossing the full width, the valvulae conniventes, look like a keyboard.
- **To-and-fro movement:** contents move back and forth without moving on. Strong, ineffective peristalsis against a blockage causes this.
- **Transition point:** dilated bowel meets collapsed bowel. The cause often lies here.
- **Collapsed colon:** supports a small bowel cause rather than a large bowel one.

## Signs of complications

- A thick bowel wall, over about 3 mm.
- Free fluid between dilated loops.
- Absent peristalsis in a segment which was moving.

Together these raise concern for strangulation or ischaemia, and need urgent surgical review.

> **The aha** Dilated, to-and-fro and a transition point: think mechanical obstruction.

> **Pitfall** Late obstruction loses peristalsis, so the bowel looks still, as in ileus. Absent movement does not exclude obstruction. Ultrasound supports the diagnosis. CT defines the cause and complications.

## Free air: look at the peritoneal stripe

A perforated viscus lets air into the peritoneal cavity. With the patient flat, it rises under the anterior abdominal wall. Ultrasound can show it, although CT is the definitive test.

1. Place a high-frequency linear probe on the right upper abdomen, over the liver.
2. Find the thin bright peritoneal stripe between the abdominal wall and the liver.
3. Free air makes that stripe brighter and thicker, with reverberation or a dirty shadow beneath it: the enhanced peritoneal stripe sign. Bowel gas sits deeper, under a normal thin stripe, inside a loop.
4. Change the angle and, if safe, the position. Free air can move.

A normal scan does not exclude a perforation, and free air is expected after recent laparoscopy or surgery. The Wavelength Pearl [Pneumoperitoneum: inspect the stripe](/learn/pneumoperitoneum-ultrasound/) shows each step.

?? A 70-year-old on steroids has sudden epigastric pain and a rigid abdomen. Over the liver, the peritoneal stripe is unusually bright, with reverberation lines beneath it which move as you tilt the probe. What do you do?
+ Treat it as a likely perforation: urgent surgical review and CT
- Diagnose bowel gas and discharge
- Repeat the scan tomorrow
- Start oral antacids and reassess in a week
: An enhanced peritoneal stripe with reverberation suggests free air. With this history and examination, perforation is likely. Ultrasound speeds up the escalation; CT and the surgeons decide the rest.

## Gas in the liver: portal or biliary

Bright echoes in the liver, sometimes with ring-down behind them, are gas. Where they lie tells you which kind. Portal blood flows into the liver and out to its edges, so **portal venous gas** reaches the periphery, within a couple of centimetres of the capsule, and moves with the flow; pulsed-wave Doppler on the portal vein shows sharp spikes. Bile drains back towards the hilum, so **pneumobilia** stays central, along the larger ducts.

Pneumobilia is usually expected after a sphincterotomy, a biliary stent or a surgical join between the bile duct and the bowel. Portal venous gas in an unwell patient with abdominal pain can signal ischaemic bowel and needs urgent CT and surgical review. The Wavelength Pearl [Liver air: portal venous gas or pneumobilia?](/learn/portal-venous-gas-pneumobilia-ultrasound/) shows both patterns.

?? An 80-year-old with atrial fibrillation has severe abdominal pain, a soft abdomen and a lactate of 6. Bright echoes reach the edge of the liver and spikes appear on the portal vein Doppler trace. What do you do?
+ Treat it as possible bowel ischaemia: urgent CT and surgical review
- Reassure: it is pneumobilia after an old ERCP
- Repeat the scan tomorrow
- Start an ERCP
: Peripheral gas with Doppler spikes is portal venous gas. With this history, mesenteric ischaemia is likely until proven otherwise.

## Ileus or obstruction

In ileus, the bowel dilates without a blockage. Peristalsis is reduced all along the bowel, with no transition point, and both small and large bowel often dilate.
