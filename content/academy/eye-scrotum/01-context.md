The eye and the testis share three features. Both are small, superficial and easy to scan with a linear probe. Both have time-critical emergencies: a retinal detachment threatening central vision, and a torsed testis losing its blood supply. And in both, the specialist decides, not the scan.

## What the bedside scan offers

- **The eye:** a look behind a small pupil, a cataract or a bleed. Ultrasound in the emergency department detects retinal detachment well. In a multicentre study by Lahham and colleagues, sensitivity was 96.9% and specificity 88.1%.
- **The scrotum:** a quick look at the cord and the testis, to support a referral. The whirlpool sign of a twisted cord is highly specific for torsion.

## What it does not offer

- A normal ocular scan does not exclude a retinal tear. Every patient with new flashes and floaters needs same-day ophthalmology assessment.
- Normal colour flow does not exclude torsion. Partial or intermittent torsion preserves flow. A clinical suspicion of torsion needs urology now.

> **The aha** In both, the scan helps the referral. It never replaces it, and it never delays it.

## Consent and dignity

Explain what you will do and why. Offer a chaperone for the scrotal scan, and record that you offered one. Keep the patient covered except for the area you scan.
