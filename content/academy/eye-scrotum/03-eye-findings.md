Three conditions cause sudden floaters or visual loss and show on ultrasound. The way each one moves tells them apart.

## Retinal detachment

- A thick, bright membrane, often folded or V-shaped.
- Tethered to the optic disc at the back, because the retina is anchored there.
- Moves little with eye movement, and stops quickly.

Ask about the central vision. A detachment which spares the macula, the centre of the retina, needs urgent surgery to keep it that way. Refer same day, by phone.

## Posterior vitreous detachment

- A thin, faint membrane, best seen with high gain.
- Not tethered to the optic disc.
- Moves freely and keeps swinging after the eye stops, like seaweed in a current.

Posterior vitreous detachment is common with age. It causes flashes and floaters and can tear the retina as it separates. Ultrasound is less sensitive for it: in the Lahham study, sensitivity was 42.5%, with specificity of 96.0%.

## Vitreous haemorrhage

- Grey, low-level echoes in the vitreous, best seen with high gain.
- Swirl like a snow globe after eye movement.
- Dense haemorrhage can hide a detachment behind it. Look carefully at the back of the globe.

> **The aha** Tethered and stiff means retina. Free and floating means vitreous. Swirling dots mean blood.

> **Pitfall** A posterior vitreous detachment or haemorrhage can hide a retinal tear. Every patient with new flashes and floaters needs same-day ophthalmology assessment.

## The optic nerve sheath: a clue to raised pressure

Behind the globe, the optic nerve runs back as a dark band inside its sheath. Raised intracranial pressure distends the sheath. Measure 3 mm behind the globe, outer edge to outer edge, in two planes in each eye, and average. Studies use cut-offs of about 5 to 6 mm in adults. A wide sheath should speed up CT and senior review; a normal one does not exclude raised pressure, and the scan must never delay either. The Wavelength Pearl [Optic nerve sheath: measure 3 mm behind the globe](/learn/optic-nerve-sheath-diameter-ultrasound/) shows each step.

## Other findings

- **Lens dislocation:** after blunt trauma, find the lens first. It normally sits just behind the iris as an oval with bright edges. Dislocated, it lies in the vitreous, often on the posterior wall, and is missing from its place. Forward dislocation into the anterior chamber can raise the eye pressure. Do not scan if globe rupture is possible. The Wavelength Pearl [Eye trauma: has the lens moved?](/learn/lens-dislocation-ultrasound/) shows each step.
- **Retrobulbar haematoma:** a dark collection behind the globe after trauma, with proptosis and a tense orbit. Lateral canthotomy is the treatment when vision is threatened. Do not delay it for imaging.
