title: "The pericardium: effusion, pleural fluid and tamponade"
minutes: 15

A black stripe behind the heart is the commonest cardiac finding in the emergency department, and the commonest to misread. Two questions follow: is it pericardial, and is it causing tamponade?

## Pericardial or pleural: find the descending aorta

In the PLAX view, the descending thoracic aorta sits behind the left atrium as a small round black circle. It is your landmark.

- **Pericardial fluid** collects behind the LV, inside the bright pericardium. It tracks towards the left atrium and runs in front of the descending aorta, between the aorta and the heart.
- **Left pleural fluid** sits behind the descending aorta. It does not pass between the aorta and the heart.
- **Both** show fluid in front of and behind the aorta, separated by the bright pericardium.

> **The aha** Find the descending aorta before you name the effusion.

## Epicardial fat

Fat on the surface of the heart sits in front of the RV, often looks grey or dark, and moves with the heart. It is common in older and heavier patients. A stripe in front of the RV only, with nothing behind the heart, is more likely fat than fluid. Free fluid in a supine patient collects behind the heart first.

## Size

Measure the depth of fluid in diastole. As a rough guide, under 1 cm is small, 1 to 2 cm moderate, and over 2 cm large. Size does not decide tamponade. A small effusion which builds quickly, after trauma or a procedure, can cause tamponade. A large chronic effusion often does not.

## Signs supporting tamponade

Tamponade happens when pressure in the pericardium stops the right heart filling. Look for:

1. **Right atrial collapse** in systole, lasting more than a third of the cardiac cycle.
2. **Right ventricular collapse** in diastole. More specific than atrial collapse.
3. **A full IVC**, over about 2 cm, which barely changes with breathing.

Tamponade is a clinical diagnosis: hypotension, tachycardia, raised jugular venous pressure, with the effusion and these signs supporting it. A hypotensive patient with an effusion and chamber collapse needs senior help and preparation for drainage now.

> **Pitfall** A plethoric IVC is common in ventilated patients and in heart failure. On its own it does not mean tamponade. Chamber collapse with a full IVC in a shocked patient does.

## Confirm in a second view

Check the subcostal view. Fluid shows as a black band between the liver and the RV. Use the apical view if the others are poor.
