---
title: "Left ventricular function: by eye and by EPSS"
minutes: 15
---

At the bedside you are sorting the LV into three groups: hyperdynamic, normal or reduced. Severely reduced function is the finding which changes management most.

## By eye

Watch the LV in at least two views. Look at three things:

- **Wall thickening.** Healthy muscle thickens by a third or more in systole.
- **Inward movement.** The walls move towards the centre of the cavity.
- **Change in cavity size.** The cavity gets clearly smaller in systole.

A hyperdynamic LV nearly closes in systole, often with the papillary muscles touching. It suggests low preload or low systemic resistance, such as sepsis or bleeding. A severely impaired LV barely changes size and its walls barely thicken.

## E-point septal separation (EPSS)

EPSS gives you one objective number. In a strong heart, the anterior mitral leaflet swings open in early diastole almost to the septum. In a weak, dilated heart, it falls short.

1. Get a good PLAX view, with the septum and posterior wall close to horizontal.
2. Switch on M-mode and place the line through the tip of the anterior mitral leaflet.
3. The leaflet traces an M shape. The first, taller peak is the E-point, early filling. The second is the A-point, atrial contraction.
4. Measure from the E-point to the septum.

Without M-mode, freeze the loop on the frame where the leaflet opens widest in early diastole and measure the shortest distance to the septum.

## Reading the number

- **About 7 mm or less.** Normal.
- **Above about 7 mm.** Supports reduced LV systolic function. The wider the gap, the weaker the ventricle tends to be.

In McKaigney and colleagues' emergency department study, an EPSS above 7 mm was 100% sensitive but only about 52% specific for severely reduced ejection fraction. A normal EPSS makes severe LV impairment unlikely. A raised EPSS needs the rest of the picture before you call the ventricle weak.

> **The aha** Measure the gap, then look at the whole ventricle. The number supports your eye, it does not replace it.

> **Pitfall** EPSS reads high without poor LV function when the leaflet cannot open freely: mitral stenosis, an aortic regurgitant jet striking the anterior leaflet, or an off-axis view. A thick septum makes it read falsely low. Regional wall motion problems at the apex do not show on EPSS.
