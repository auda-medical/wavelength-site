?? A breathless patient has a normal focused cardiac scan, with a normal-sized right ventricle. What does this tell you about pulmonary embolism?
@ 01-context
+ It remains possible and needs its own assessment
- It is excluded
- It is excluded if the IVC is also small
- It is excluded if left ventricular function is normal
: Focused cardiac ultrasound answers a few yes or no questions. A normal RV makes massive pulmonary embolism less likely in a shocked patient, but a smaller embolism leaves the heart looking normal. The same applies to aortic dissection and acute coronary syndrome. Follow your usual pathway.

?? Which question is outside the scope of focused cardiac ultrasound?
@ 01-context
+ Grading the severity of aortic stenosis
- Is there a pericardial effusion?
- Is the right ventricle dilated?
- Is left ventricular function severely reduced?
: Focused cardiac ultrasound covers LV function, pericardial effusion, RV size, volume status and cardiac activity. Grading valve disease needs a formal echocardiogram with Doppler measurements by a trained echocardiographer.

?? From the parasternal long-axis view, how do you obtain the parasternal short-axis view?
@ 02-views
+ Rotate the probe 90° clockwise, so the marker points to the patient's left shoulder
- Slide the probe down to the apex beat
- Move the probe below the xiphoid
- Rotate the probe 180°
: Rotating 90° clockwise from PLAX turns the beam across the LV, which then appears round. Tilting moves the slice between the mitral valve, papillary muscle and apical levels. The apex beat is the window for the apical four-chamber view.

?? In the subcostal view you see bowel gas and no heart. What is the best adjustment?
@ 02-views
+ Lay the probe flatter against the abdomen and aim at the left shoulder
- Press harder below the ribs
- Hold the probe upright like a pen
- Reduce the depth
: The heart sits above and behind the costal margin. A flat probe sends the beam under the ribs to the heart. Pressing harder causes pain and tenses the abdomen. An upright probe points the beam at the spine. Deeper settings help, not shallower.

?? Where do you place the M-mode line to measure E-point septal separation?
@ 03-lv-function
+ Through the tip of the anterior mitral leaflet in the parasternal long-axis view
- Through the aortic valve in the parasternal short-axis view
- Through the mitral annulus in the apical four-chamber view
- Through the right ventricle in the subcostal view
: EPSS measures how close the anterior mitral leaflet comes to the septum in early diastole. The parasternal long-axis view shows both in line with the beam, and M-mode through the leaflet tip records the E-point clearly.

?? An adult has an EPSS of 15 mm on a good view with normal valves. What does this suggest?
@ 03-lv-function
+ Reduced left ventricular systolic function
- Normal left ventricular function
- A hyperdynamic left ventricle
- Cardiac tamponade
: An EPSS above about 7 mm supports reduced LV systolic function, and 15 mm is well above this. In a hyperdynamic heart the leaflet comes very close to the septum. Confirm the finding with your visual assessment of the whole ventricle.

?? Which condition makes EPSS overestimate left ventricular impairment?
@ 03-lv-function
+ Aortic regurgitation with the jet striking the anterior mitral leaflet
- Sinus tachycardia
- Hypovolaemia
- A small pericardial effusion
: A regurgitant aortic jet strikes the anterior mitral leaflet in diastole and stops it opening fully, so the gap to the septum widens without LV impairment. Mitral stenosis does the same. A thick septum makes EPSS read falsely low.

?? In the parasternal long-axis view, black fluid lies between the heart and the descending aorta. What is it?
@ 04-pericardium
+ A pericardial effusion
- A left pleural effusion
- Epicardial fat
- The coronary sinus
: The pericardium wraps the heart in front of the descending aorta, so pericardial fluid runs between the heart and the aorta. Pleural fluid lies outside the pericardium and behind the aorta.

?? You see a grey stripe in front of the right ventricle only, with nothing behind the heart. What is the most likely cause?
@ 04-pericardium
+ Epicardial fat
- A large pericardial effusion
- A left pleural effusion
- Pneumothorax
: Epicardial fat sits in front of the heart and moves with it. Free pericardial fluid collects behind the heart first in a supine patient. A stripe in front only is more likely fat. Confirm in a second view.

?? Which finding is the most specific sign supporting cardiac tamponade?
@ 04-pericardium
+ Right ventricular collapse in diastole
- A pericardial effusion over 2 cm deep
- A large inferior vena cava
- Sinus tachycardia
: Right ventricular collapse in diastole shows the pericardial pressure exceeds the filling pressure of the RV. Effusion size does not decide tamponade: a small effusion which builds quickly can cause it. A large IVC supports tamponade but is common in other conditions.

?? In the apical four-chamber view, which finding shows a dilated right ventricle?
@ 05-right-heart-venous
+ The right ventricle is as large as or larger than the left ventricle
- The right ventricle is half the size of the left ventricle
- The right ventricle wall moves inwards in systole
- The septum bows towards the right ventricle
: The RV is normally smaller than two thirds of the LV in the apical four-chamber view. An RV equal to or larger than the LV is dilated. When the RV is under pressure, the septum bows towards the LV, making a D shape in short axis.

?? How do you make sure the vessel you are measuring is the inferior vena cava?
@ 05-right-heart-venous
+ Follow it from the subcostal view as it runs through the liver into the right atrium
- Choose the vessel with the thickest walls
- Choose the vessel which pulses most strongly
- Look for branches running forwards from it
: The IVC is the vessel which enters the right atrium. Thick, bright, pulsatile walls and branches running forwards, the coeliac trunk and superior mesenteric artery, belong to the aorta.

?? What marks the top of the venous column when you scan the internal jugular vein?
@ 05-right-heart-venous
+ The point where the vein tapers and its walls meet
- The point where the vein looks widest
- The level of the carotid bifurcation
- The point where the vein crosses the clavicle
: Below the top of the column, blood fills the vein. Above it, the vein is empty and its walls meet. The collapse point is the ultrasound equivalent of the top of the visible pulsation in a clinical JVP. Vein width varies with position, probe pressure and breathing.

?? During a cardiac arrest, how do you use focused cardiac ultrasound?
@ 06-pitfalls-documentation
+ Place the probe before the pulse check, record a short clip in the pause, and review it during compressions
- Pause compressions until you have a clear view
- Scan continuously during compressions in the parasternal view
- Stop resuscitation if no cardiac activity is seen
: Ultrasound must not lengthen pauses in compressions. Position the probe in the subcostal view beforehand, record no more than 10 seconds, and interpret the clip once compressions restart. Ultrasound findings alone do not decide when to stop resuscitation.

?? You could not obtain an apical four-chamber view. How do you record this?
@ 06-pitfalls-documentation
+ List the apical view as not obtained, and the findings from the views you did get
- Leave the apical view out of the note
- Record the right ventricle as normal size
- Record the scan as normal
: Documenting the views obtained and not obtained shows the limits of your scan to the next clinician. Recording a structure as normal when you did not see it well is the most common cause of a confident wrong report.
