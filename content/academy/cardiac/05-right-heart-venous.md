The right side of the heart and the large veins tell you about pulmonary embolism, fluid status and venous congestion. No single sign answers the question. Put them together.

## Right ventricular size

- In the apical four-chamber view the RV is normally smaller than two thirds of the LV. An RV as large as or larger than the LV is dilated.
- In PSAX, a D-shaped LV, with the septum flattened towards it, shows the RV under pressure or volume load.
- In a shocked patient, a dilated RV with a small, vigorous LV raises the question of massive pulmonary embolism. A thick RV wall, over about 5 mm, suggests a chronic cause, such as pulmonary hypertension.

### Build the pattern

One enlarged ventricle is a clue. Add three questions before you act on it:

1. **Is the septum flat?** A D-shaped LV in short axis shows the RV is under pressure.
2. **Is the RV wall thin?** A thin wall fits an acute rise in pressure. A thick wall points to chronic disease, such as COPD or pulmonary hypertension, which mimics acute strain.
3. **Do the veins and lungs agree?** A plethoric IVC, a non-compressible femoral or popliteal vein and clear lungs with A-lines all make pulmonary embolism more likely.

A normal RV does not exclude pulmonary embolism, because most small emboli leave the RV unchanged.

## The IVC: follow it into the right atrium

The aorta lies close to the IVC and looks similar in long axis. Make sure you have the right vessel.

1. Start in the subcostal four-chamber view.
2. Move the probe until the right atrium sits in the centre of the screen.
3. Rotate 90° so the marker points to the patient's head.
4. Tilt slightly to the patient's right until a thin-walled vessel runs through the liver into the right atrium.
5. Measure about 2 cm from the atrium, just beyond the hepatic vein, at a right angle to the vessel.

The aorta has thick, bright walls, pulses, gives off branches running forwards, and never reaches the heart.

- **A small IVC which collapses with a sniff** suggests a low right atrial pressure.
- **A large IVC, over about 2 cm, which barely changes** suggests a high right atrial pressure.

The IVC adds context. It does not write the fluid prescription. Read it with the heart and the lungs:

- **Plethoric IVC with a dilated RV:** right heart strain.
- **Plethoric IVC with bilateral B-lines:** raised filling pressures and lung water. More fluid risks harm.
- **Small, collapsing IVC with a vigorous LV and clear lungs:** fits hypovolaemia or vasodilatation. Give fluid in measured boluses and reassess after each one.

Before you read the size, look inside the vein. Thrombus extending from the leg or pelvic veins, or around a femoral line, and tumour from the kidney or liver also make the IVC wide and still. Fresh clot can be almost black, so add colour. A mobile clot reaching the right atrium is an emergency. ## The ultrasound JVP: find the collapse point

When the neck exam is difficult, ultrasound shows the internal jugular vein (IJV) directly.

1. Sit the patient at 30 to 45°, head neutral.
2. Use the linear probe on the right side of the neck, in transverse, with almost no pressure.
3. Find the IJV beside the carotid artery just above the clavicle, then slide up the neck.
4. Stop where the vein tapers and its walls meet. This is the collapse point, the top of the venous column.
5. Confirm in longitudinal, then estimate its vertical height above the sternal angle.

A collapse point near the clavicle suggests a low column. A vein full right up to the jaw suggests a high right-sided filling pressure.

> **The aha** Find the top of the column, not the size of the vein. Find the right atrium, then the IVC.

> **Pitfall** IVC and IJV size depend on breathing effort, ventilation and abdominal pressure. Positive-pressure ventilation fills both. Probe pressure collapses the IJV and gives a false low reading. Use them as one part of the picture, never as a single test for giving fluid.
