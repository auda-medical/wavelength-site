Five patients from the emergency department. Read each case, choose your answer, then read the explanation. These cases do not count towards your score.

## Case 1: the breathless man

A 68-year-old man has two weeks of worsening breathlessness and ankle swelling. Blood pressure 105/70. In PLAX, the LV is large and its walls barely move. M-mode through the anterior mitral leaflet shows an EPSS of 18 mm. There is no effusion. The IVC measures 2.4 cm and barely changes with breathing.

?? What do these findings support?
+ Reduced LV systolic function with a high right-sided filling pressure
- Hypovolaemia
- Cardiac tamponade
- A normal heart
: A large, poorly moving LV with an EPSS well above 7 mm supports reduced LV systolic function. A large IVC with little change on breathing suggests a high right atrial pressure. There is no effusion, so tamponade is not the cause. Large volumes of fluid carry risk in this patient.

## Case 2: fluid behind the heart

A 74-year-old woman with lung cancer is breathless. In PLAX you see a black space behind the heart. It lies behind the descending aorta, and nothing passes between the aorta and the left atrium.

?? What is the fluid?
+ A left pleural effusion
- A pericardial effusion
- Epicardial fat
- An aortic dissection
: Fluid behind the descending aorta, which does not pass in front of it, lies in the left pleural space. Pericardial fluid runs between the heart and the aorta. Scan the left lung base to confirm it and judge its size.

## Case 3: the hypotensive patient after a procedure

A 59-year-old man becomes hypotensive two hours after a pacemaker insertion. Blood pressure 78/50, heart rate 125. The subcostal view shows a 1.2 cm pericardial effusion. The RV free wall moves inwards in diastole. The IVC is 2.5 cm and does not change with breathing.

?? What is the most important next step?
+ Call for senior help and prepare for urgent pericardial drainage
- Give a 2 litre fluid bolus and rescan in an hour
- Arrange an outpatient echocardiogram
- Treat as heart failure with diuretics
: A moderate effusion after a procedure, with RV collapse in diastole, a plethoric IVC and shock, supports tamponade. A rapidly developing effusion causes tamponade at a small size. This needs senior help and drainage now. A small fluid bolus can support filling while you prepare, but delay risks arrest.

## Case 4: the large right ventricle

A 45-year-old woman collapses four days after a long flight. Blood pressure 82/55, oxygen saturation 86% on air. In the apical four-chamber view the RV is larger than the LV. In PSAX the LV is D-shaped. The LV is small and moves vigorously. There is no effusion.

?? What does this pattern suggest?
+ Acute RV strain, raising the question of massive pulmonary embolism
- Cardiac tamponade
- Reduced LV systolic function
- A normal finding after a collapse
: A dilated RV with a D-shaped LV shows the RV under pressure. In a shocked patient with this history, massive pulmonary embolism leads the list. The LV is small because the RV cannot push blood through the lungs. Escalate immediately and follow your pulmonary embolism pathway.

## Case 5: the neck vein

A 72-year-old man is breathless. His neck is short and you cannot see the JVP. You scan the right IJV with the linear probe, the patient at 45°. Just above the clavicle the vein looks closed. You notice you are pressing firmly. You lift the probe until it barely touches the gel, and the vein fills to well above the angle of the jaw.

?? What is the right reading?
+ The venous column is high. The first view was falsely low from probe pressure
- The venous column is low, as first seen
- The test is invalid in short necks
- The vein is thrombosed
: Probe pressure collapses the IJV and makes the column look short. With almost no pressure, the vein stays full to the jaw, which suggests a high right-sided filling pressure. A thrombosed vein does not fill and empty with light pressure, and contains echoes.
