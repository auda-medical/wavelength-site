title: "Context: the focused questions"
minutes: 10
---

Focused cardiac ultrasound (FoCUS) is not echocardiography. It answers a small number of yes or no questions at the bedside, in minutes, in a patient who is breathless, hypotensive or in cardiac arrest. Each answer changes what you do next.

## The five questions

1. **Is the left ventricle (LV) contracting well, or poorly?** Poor function points towards cardiogenic shock or heart failure, and changes how much fluid you give.
2. **Is there a pericardial effusion?** And if there is, are there signs supporting tamponade?
3. **Is the right ventricle (RV) dilated?** A large RV in a shocked patient raises the question of massive pulmonary embolism.
4. **How full is the venous system?** The inferior vena cava (IVC) and the jugular veins show whether the right-sided filling pressure is likely high or low.
5. **Is there cardiac activity?** In cardiac arrest, the subcostal view during the pulse check looks for movement and for reversible causes.

## What FoCUS does not do

- It does not grade valve disease, measure ejection fraction precisely or assess diastolic function.
- It does not exclude a pulmonary embolism, an aortic dissection or an acute coronary syndrome. A normal scan leaves all three possible.
- It does not replace a formal echocardiogram when the question needs one.

> **The aha** Ask the question before you pick up the probe. A focused scan answers a focused question.

## Where it fits

FoCUS sits alongside the history, examination, ECG and blood tests. In the undifferentiated breathless or shocked patient, combine it with lung ultrasound and the IVC. Together they separate the main causes faster than any one test alone. The 2014 international recommendations support FoCUS for LV function, pericardial effusion, RV size and volume status, in trained hands.

## Training and governance

Learn the views under supervision, keep a logbook, and record your scans. Your department may follow a pathway such as FUSIC Heart, or the RCEM curriculum's point-of-care ultrasound appendix. Know which findings you are signed off to act on. When you are unsure, say so in the notes and ask for a formal echocardiogram.

> **Pitfall** A confident report from a single poor view is the commonest error. If you cannot see a structure well, say so. "Not assessed" is a safe answer.
