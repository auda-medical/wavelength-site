---
title: "Necrotising fasciitis: look below the cobblestones"
minutes: 10
---

Necrotising fasciitis spreads along the deep fascia faster than the skin changes show. Early on, it looks like cellulitis. Pain out of proportion to the redness, a rising heart rate and a patient who looks unwell are the clues. Surgical debridement is both the diagnosis and the treatment, and every hour of delay raises mortality.

Ultrasound has a narrow role: it can raise your concern and speed the surgical call. It cannot rule the diagnosis out.

## STAFF

The STAFF mnemonic lists the findings to look for:

- **S**ubcutaneous **T**hickening: thick, bright fat, often with cobblestoning. Cellulitis causes this too.
- **A**ir: bright spots in the soft tissue with grey, dirty shadowing or ring-down behind them.
- **F**ascial **F**luid: a dark layer of fluid lying along the deep fascia.

In an emergency department study of patients with suspected necrotising fasciitis, diffuse thickening of the soft tissue with more than 4 mm of fluid along the deep fascia had a sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 93%.

## The scan

1. Scan normal skin away from the redness first, for comparison.
2. Scan the most painful area, then beyond the edge of the redness. Fascial fluid often extends past the skin changes.
3. Follow the deep fascia, the bright line between fat and muscle, along its length.
4. Measure any fluid lying on the fascia.
5. Look for gas, and ask about wounds, injections and recent surgery which explain it.

> **The aha** Cobblestones sit in the fat. The danger sits on the fascia.

## What to do with the finding

- **Fascial fluid or gas with a worrying clinical picture:** call the surgical team now, start broad-spectrum antibiotics as per your trust's guideline, and resuscitate.
- **A normal scan with a worrying clinical picture:** the same. Ultrasound misses early disease.
- **Do not wait** for CT or MRI before calling the surgeons. Imaging must never delay theatre.

> **Pitfall** Ultrasound cannot safely exclude necrotising fasciitis. Use it to escalate faster, never to reassure.

## Case

A 54-year-old man with diabetes has a red, swollen thigh and severe pain which needs repeated morphine. His heart rate is 118. Ultrasound shows thickened, cobblestoned fat, a 6 mm layer of fluid along the deep fascia extending beyond the redness, and two bright spots with dirty shadowing in the fat. He has had no injections or wounds.

?? What is the next step?
+ Call the surgical team now for exploration, and start broad-spectrum antibiotics
- Treat as cellulitis and review in 48 hours
- Arrange an outpatient MRI
- Aspirate the fascial fluid for culture before treatment
