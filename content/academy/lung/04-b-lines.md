## What a B-line is

A B-line is a bright vertical line which:

1. Starts at the pleural line.
2. Runs to the bottom of the screen without fading.
3. Erases the A-lines it crosses.
4. Moves with lung sliding.

Short vertical lines which fade after a few centimetres are not B-lines. They are often called comet tails, and are normal.

## How many count

- One or two B-lines in a view are normal, especially at the bases.
- Three or more B-lines between two ribs make that zone positive.
- When B-lines merge into a white curtain, the lung is very wet.

## Interstitial syndrome

Two or more positive zones on each side make a bilateral interstitial pattern. In a breathless patient, the commonest cause is cardiogenic pulmonary oedema.

Other causes include pneumonitis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, pulmonary fibrosis and contusion.

## Telling the causes apart

- **Cardiogenic oedema:** B-lines spread evenly on both sides, with a smooth, thin pleural line and preserved sliding. Look at the heart and the IVC.
- **ARDS and pneumonitis:** patchy B-lines, spared areas, a thickened, irregular pleural line, small subpleural consolidations, reduced sliding.
- **Fibrosis:** B-lines with a thick, irregular pleural line, mainly at the bases, which do not change with treatment.
- **Focal B-lines in one area:** pneumonia, contusion or infarction.

## Map, then compare like with like

Record each zone on both sides as clear, focal, patchy or diffuse, with the pleural line smooth or irregular. That map is more useful than a precise count. The number of B-lines changes with the probe, frequency, focus, gain and the machine's processing, so when you repeat the scan to follow treatment, use the same zones and the same settings.

> **Pitfall** B-lines exclude pneumothorax under the probe, but they do not diagnose heart failure on their own. Combine them with the heart, the IVC and the history.
