Consolidated lung has lost its air, so the beam passes through it. It looks like liver: solid, grey and textured. This is called hepatisation.

## The scan

1. Use the curvilinear probe for the bases and deeper consolidation, the linear probe for small areas near the pleura.
2. Scan the area of concern in both planes, marker to the head in long axis.
3. Find the tissue-like lung. Its deep edge is often ragged where it meets aerated lung: the shred sign.
4. Hold still and watch the bright air bronchograms through two or three breaths.

## Dynamic or static

- **Dynamic air bronchograms.** Bright dots and lines move outwards as the patient breathes in. Air is still flowing through an open airway. This strongly supports pneumonia.
- **Static air bronchograms.** The dots stay still. The airway is blocked. Common in resorptive atelectasis, from a mucus plug or an obstructed bronchus, and seen in some pneumonias.
- **Fluid bronchograms.** Black branching tubes with no colour flow suggest airways filled with fluid, seen in post-obstructive pneumonia.

Colour Doppler shows branching vessels inside consolidated lung. Absent flow in a wedge-shaped area at the pleura raises the question of infarction.

> **The aha** Don't just see air bronchograms. Watch them breathe.

## Compressive atelectasis

Lung squashed by a large effusion looks consolidated and floats or flaps in the fluid. Its bronchograms are few. It re-expands when the fluid drains.

> **Pitfall** Static air bronchograms do not exclude pneumonia. Lung ultrasound only sees consolidation which reaches the pleura, so a normal scan does not exclude a central pneumonia.
