The chest and abdominal walls take their nerve supply from the thoracic spinal nerves. Each nerve leaves the spine, gives a dorsal ramus to the back, and runs forward as an intercostal nerve. It gives a lateral cutaneous branch at the side of the chest and ends as an anterior cutaneous branch near the midline.

Plane blocks place a volume of local anaesthetic between muscle layers where these nerves, or their branches, run. Where you inject decides which branches you reach:

- **Erector spinae plane block:** close to the spine, reaching the dorsal rami and, to a variable extent, the ventral rami over several levels.
- **Serratus anterior plane block:** at the side of the chest, reaching the lateral cutaneous branches. Covered in the Academy module on head, chest wall and shoulder blocks.
- **Rectus sheath block:** at the front of the abdomen, reaching the anterior cutaneous branches near the midline.

## Choosing a chest wall block

- **Lateral rib fractures:** the serratus anterior plane block has the strongest emergency department evidence, from the SABRE trial.
- **Posterior rib fractures near the spine:** the erector spinae plane block reaches where the serratus block does not.

## Plane blocks are volume blocks

Plane blocks rely on spread, which needs volume, often 20 to 30 mL per side. Volume brings dose. Bilateral blocks double it. The dose calculation is the most important safety step.

## What this module is, and is not

This module teaches the theory: the safety system, the anatomy on the screen, the needle path and the evidence. It does not make you competent to perform a block. Hands-on skill comes from supervised practice under your trust's governance.

## Who should not have a block

- A patient who declines, or who cannot consent and does not need a block in their best interests.
- Infection at the injection site.
- Allergy to local anaesthetic.
- A block which would mask an evolving problem, such as abdominal wall pain hiding a surgical abdomen, without a clear diagnosis and plan.
