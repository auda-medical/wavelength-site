The Wavelength Pearl [Rectus sheath block: behind the muscle, in front of the sheath](/learn/rectus-sheath-block/) summarises the technique.

## Anatomy

Rectus abdominis lies in a sheath on each side of the midline. Above the arcuate line, the sheath has an anterior and a posterior layer. The anterior cutaneous branches of the lower thoracic nerves, roughly T7 to T11, enter the sheath from the side and run between the muscle and the posterior layer before turning forward through the muscle to the skin.

The **arcuate line** lies about a third of the way from the umbilicus to the pubic symphysis. Below it, the posterior sheath ends, and only thin transversalis fascia lies between the muscle and the peritoneum.

The **inferior epigastric vessels** run on the deep surface of rectus abdominis, in the lower abdomen, and the superior epigastric vessels in the upper abdomen.

## On the screen

- **Rectus abdominis:** an oval muscle between two bright lines.
- **Anterior and posterior sheath:** the bright lines above and below the muscle.
- **Peritoneum:** a bright line beneath the posterior sheath, with bowel moving beneath it.
- **Epigastric vessels:** small circles on the back of the muscle, with flow on Doppler.

## Technique

1. Supine.
2. Linear probe transverse, lateral to the umbilicus, above the arcuate line.
3. Identify the muscle, the two sheaths and the peritoneum. Use Doppler to find the epigastric vessels.
4. In-plane needle from the lateral side, through the muscle, to just above the posterior sheath.
5. Inject 1 to 2 mL and watch the fluid separate the muscle from the posterior sheath.
6. Inject the rest, often 10 to 20 mL per side, checking the total dose.
7. For a midline wound, repeat on the other side.

## Coverage and uses

The block numbs the skin and muscle of the central abdominal wall around the umbilicus. It does not treat visceral pain. Most of the evidence comes from operating theatres, for midline and umbilical incisions in adults and children. In emergency care it may help with midline wound pain or procedures on the central abdominal wall, under local governance.

## Risks

- **Masking a surgical abdomen.** Be sure the pain comes from the wall before you block.
- **Peritoneal puncture or bowel injury**, more likely below the arcuate line or with the tip out of view.
- **Vascular puncture and haematoma** of the epigastric vessels.
- **Local anaesthetic toxicity**, especially with bilateral blocks.

> **The aha** The posterior sheath is the floor. Fluid peeling the muscle off it confirms the plane.

> **Pitfall** Below the arcuate line there is no posterior sheath to stop the needle. Stay above it.
