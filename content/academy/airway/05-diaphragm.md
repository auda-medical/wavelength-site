title: "The diaphragm: watch the sniff"
minutes: 12
---

The diaphragm does most of the work of breathing. Weakness or paralysis after surgery, a nerve block, trauma, neuromuscular disease or prolonged ventilation causes breathlessness and failure to wean. Ultrasound shows how it moves.

## The scan

1. Use the curvilinear or phased-array probe.
2. Right side: place the probe below the right costal margin, between the mid-clavicular and anterior axillary lines, and aim up and back towards the dome of the diaphragm. Use the liver as a window.
3. Left side: use the spleen as a window, from a lower intercostal or subcostal position. The left side is harder to see.
4. Find the diaphragm: the bright curved line deep to the liver or spleen.
5. Place the M-mode line through the dome, as close to perpendicular to its movement as you can.
6. Record quiet breathing, then a sharp sniff, then a deep breath.

## Which way is normal

The probe sits below the diaphragm and looks up at it. On inspiration the diaphragm contracts and moves down, towards the abdomen and towards the probe.

- **On the screen:** the diaphragm moves up, towards the probe, on inspiration.
- **On M-mode:** the trace rises with each breath in and falls with each breath out.

## Reading the view

- **Normal:** the diaphragm moves towards the probe on inspiration, similar on both sides.
- **Weak:** the movement is small, and smaller than the other side.
- **Paralysed:** no movement on quiet breathing. On a sniff, the diaphragm moves the wrong way, away from the probe and down on the screen. This is paradoxical movement: the negative pressure in the chest sucks the paralysed diaphragm upwards.

Boussuges and colleagues published reference values for diaphragmatic excursion on M-mode in healthy adults, with women moving slightly less than men. In practice, compare the two sides using the same manoeuvre.

> **The aha** Ask for a sniff and watch the direction. Towards the probe is normal. Away from the probe is paradoxical.

> **Pitfall** Poor effort, pain and unclear instructions all reduce movement. In a ventilated patient, the ventilator pushes the diaphragm down passively, so movement does not prove the muscle is working. Assess during spontaneous breaths.
