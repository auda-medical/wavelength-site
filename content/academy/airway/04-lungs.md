title: "The lungs after intubation"
minutes: 10
---

Once the tube is in the trachea, two problems remain: the tube may sit in a main bronchus, and positive-pressure ventilation may cause or enlarge a pneumothorax. Lung ultrasound checks both.

## The scan

1. Use the linear probe, head to foot across two ribs, in the anterior chest on each side.
2. Find the pleural line between the rib shadows, the bat sign.
3. Watch for lung sliding with each ventilator breath.
4. If sliding is absent, look for B-lines and the lung pulse, then for a lung point.

## Reading the view

- **Sliding on both sides:** both lungs are ventilated, and there is no pneumothorax under the probe on either side.
- **Sliding on the right, lung pulse on the left:** the left lung lies against the chest wall but is not ventilated. The tube most likely sits in the right main bronchus. Withdraw it slightly and rescan.
- **No sliding, no lung pulse, no B-lines, A-lines only on one side:** pneumothorax likely. A lung point confirms it.

> **The aha** A lung pulse means the lung is touching the chest wall. No sliding with a lung pulse means not ventilated, not pneumothorax.

> **Pitfall** A tension pneumothorax in a ventilated patient is a clinical diagnosis. Rising airway pressures, falling saturations and hypotension need decompression, not a search for a lung point.

## After the scan

Ultrasound shows which lungs are ventilated. A chest X-ray confirms tube depth, line positions and the overall picture, and remains part of routine post-intubation care.
