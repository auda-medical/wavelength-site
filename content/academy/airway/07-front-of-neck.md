Every difficult airway plan ends with front-of-neck access through the cricothyroid membrane. In a slim neck you can feel it. In obesity, neck swelling, previous surgery or radiotherapy, palpation often misses it, and ultrasound finds it much more often. The time to find it is during preparation, while the patient is oxygenating.

## The scan

1. Position the head and neck as your airway plan allows. Use a high-frequency linear probe, plenty of gel and a light hand, so you do not distort the soft tissue.
2. **Transverse, from the top.** Find the thyroid cartilage: a larger, angular, dark structure with a bright line deep to it where air meets the lining of the airway.
3. Slide down to the cricoid cartilage: a smaller, rounded dark ring. Below it, the tracheal rings form a string of dark beads.
4. **Long axis in the midline.** Turn the probe. The cricothyroid membrane is the soft-tissue gap between the bottom of the thyroid cartilage and the top of the cricoid.
5. Mark the centre of the membrane and the midline on the skin, with the neck in the position you will use for the procedure.
6. Recheck the mark if the head or neck is moved.

## Findings

- **Thyroid cartilage:** angular, larger, cranial.
- **Cricoid cartilage:** rounded, smaller, caudal.
- **Membrane:** the gap between them, with the bright air line beneath.
- **Vessels:** colour Doppler shows any vessel crossing the membrane before you plan an incision.

> **The aha** Ultrasound belongs before the emergency, not between failed oxygenation and rescue.

> **Pitfall** A neat image is not a usable skin mark. Mark the skin with the neck positioned for the procedure, and tell the team the mark may need checking if the patient is moved.

## Where it fits

Pre-identification of the membrane is part of preparing for an anticipated difficult airway. It does not replace the plan, the equipment, the trained assistant or the scalpel technique. In a can't intubate, can't oxygenate emergency, proceed to front-of-neck access without fetching or using a scanner.

## Case

A 54-year-old with a body mass index of 47 needs intubation for a planned emergency procedure. You cannot feel the laryngeal cartilages. The patient is oxygenating well on high-flow oxygen.

?? What is the best use of ultrasound here?
+ Find and mark the cricothyroid membrane now, as part of the airway plan
- Wait and scan the neck only if oxygenation fails
- Skip the airway plan, because ultrasound makes front-of-neck access safe
- Scan only the stomach
: In an obese neck, palpation is unreliable. Marking the membrane during preparation, while oxygenation is stable, means the team knows where to cut if rescue becomes necessary. Ultrasound must never delay rescue once oxygenation has failed.
