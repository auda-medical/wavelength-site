Intubation and ventilation carry a handful of avoidable harms: aspiration of stomach contents, a tube in the oesophagus, a tube too deep, a pneumothorax, and a weak diaphragm which stops a patient coming off the ventilator. Ultrasound answers one question about each, at the bedside, in under a minute.

## The four scans

1. **The stomach.** Before sedation or intubation: is the stomach empty, full of clear fluid, or full of solid food?
2. **The tube.** During intubation: did the tube go into the trachea or the oesophagus?
3. **The lungs.** After intubation: are both lungs ventilated, and is there a pneumothorax?
4. **The diaphragm.** In the breathless or ventilated patient: is the diaphragm moving, and in the right direction?

## What ultrasound adds, and what it does not

- It shows anatomy directly, at the bedside, with no radiation.
- It does not replace waveform capnography for tube confirmation. No trace means wrong place.
- It does not replace a chest X-ray for tube depth or line position when those matter.
- Gastric ultrasound informs the aspiration-risk assessment. It is not a "safe to sedate" test on its own.

> **The aha** Each scan answers one question. Ask it before you pick up the probe.

> **Pitfall** Never delay oxygenation or a critical airway action to get an image. Scan when it does not slow the resuscitation.
