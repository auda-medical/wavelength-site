## Case 1: the full stomach

A 45-year-old with a dislocated hip needs procedural sedation. He last ate four hours ago and has had opioids. Gastric ultrasound in the right lateral position shows a distended antrum with bright, mixed contents which hide the back wall.

?? How do you interpret this?
+ Solid contents. Treat as a full stomach and discuss timing and technique with a senior
- An empty stomach, safe to proceed as planned
- Clear fluid only, of no concern
- A normal finding after four hours
: Bright, mixed contents which hide the back wall are solid food. Opioids and pain slow gastric emptying, so fasting time alone misleads. A full stomach changes the approach to sedation and airway protection.

## Case 2: the arrest

During a cardiac arrest, a patient is intubated. The capnography trace is low and flat. Neck ultrasound during tube passage shows one air-mucosa interface in the midline, with a flutter as the tube passes, and the oesophagus stays collapsed.

?? What does the scan suggest?
+ The tube passed into the trachea. Low flow in cardiac arrest explains the flat trace, so keep reassessing capnography
- The tube is in the oesophagus
- The tube is in the right main bronchus
- The scan is invalid during cardiac arrest
: One tract in the midline, with no second air shadow, shows tracheal placement. Low pulmonary blood flow in arrest gives a low or flat trace. Ultrasound adds to capnography but does not replace it. If in doubt, take it out.

## Case 3: the falling saturations

Ten minutes after intubation, oxygen saturation drifts down. Airway pressures are stable. The right chest shows sliding. The left shows no sliding, but the pleural line flickers with each heartbeat.

?? What is the most likely cause?
+ Right main bronchus intubation
- Left tension pneumothorax
- Oesophageal intubation
- Left pleural effusion
: A lung pulse shows the left lung lies against the chest wall, so there is no pneumothorax under the probe. The left lung is not being ventilated. Withdraw the tube slightly and rescan.

## Case 4: after the block

A patient is breathless after an interscalene block for shoulder surgery. On the right, the diaphragm moves towards the probe on a sniff. On the left, the side of the block, the diaphragm moves away from the probe on a sniff.

?? What does this show?
+ Left hemidiaphragm paralysis, with paradoxical movement
- Normal movement on both sides
- Right hemidiaphragm paralysis
- A left pneumothorax
: Interscalene blocks often spread to the phrenic nerve. A paralysed hemidiaphragm is sucked upwards on a sniff, moving away from the probe. Supportive care and monitoring usually suffice until the block wears off.
