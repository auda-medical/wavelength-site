## The idea

Fascia iliaca covers iliacus and psoas. The femoral nerve and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve both run beneath it. A volume of local anaesthetic placed under the fascia, away from the nerves, spreads along the plane and reaches them. The block was first described in children in 1989 as a landmark technique with two "pops". Ultrasound now lets you see the fascia and the spread.

Because it relies on volume, 30 to 40 mL, the dose is the main safety concern. In a frail 50 kg patient, the maximum safe dose may decide the concentration you use.

## Infra-inguinal approach

1. Linear probe transverse below the inguinal ligament. Find the femoral artery and nerve.
2. Slide lateral until fascia iliaca lies on iliacus, about a third of the way from the artery to the anterior superior iliac spine.
3. In-plane needle from lateral to medial, aiming under the fascia.
4. Inject 2 to 3 mL and confirm fluid separating the fascia from iliacus.
5. Inject the rest, watching the spread medially towards the nerve.

**Coverage:** the femoral nerve reliably, the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve often, the obturator nerve inconsistently.

## Supra-inguinal approach

Described in 2011, this approach places the injection above the inguinal ligament, inside the pelvis, under the same fascia.

1. Linear probe in a sagittal plane, medial to the anterior superior iliac spine, above the ligament.
2. Identify iliacus in the iliac fossa, fascia iliaca over it, and the abdominal wall muscles and sartorius above, forming a bow tie shape.
3. Colour Doppler finds the deep circumflex iliac artery just above the fascia, which marks the level.
4. In-plane needle from the lower end of the probe, aiming upwards under the fascia.
5. Confirm fluid running towards the head along iliacus, then inject the rest.

**Coverage:** more consistent spread to the femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves. A trial after hip replacement showed reduced morphine use.

## Choosing between them

- The **infra-inguinal** approach is simpler, widely taught and the basis of many nurse-led pathways.
- The **supra-inguinal** approach gives more reliable spread but needs more confidence with the sagittal view and the needle angle.

Use the approach your trust's pathway supports, and the one you are trained in.

## Risks

- Local anaesthetic toxicity, because of the volume.
- Quadriceps weakness, as with a femoral block.
- Failure, if the fluid stays above the fascia.
- Entry into the abdomen with the supra-inguinal approach, if the needle advances beyond the curve of the iliac fossa.

> **The aha** You do not need to see a nerve. You need to see the fascia lift off iliacus.

> **Pitfall** Fluid gathering around sartorius or in the fat means the tip is above the fascia. Stop and reposition before you give the volume.
