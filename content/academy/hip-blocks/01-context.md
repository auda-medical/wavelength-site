title: "Why the hip fracture patient needs a block"

A fractured neck of femur is the commonest serious injury in older people. Most patients are frail, many have dementia or delirium, and many take anticoagulants. Every movement hurts: the transfer from the ambulance trolley, the X-ray, the trip to the toilet. Opioids help, but in this group they cause drowsiness, confusion, constipation and falls.

A nerve block treats the pain at its source. UK guidance, including the Association of Anaesthetists' hip fracture guideline, supports a nerve block before surgery, and many UK departments give it in the emergency department through nurse-led or practitioner-led fascia iliaca block pathways.

This module covers four blocks:

- **Femoral nerve block:** a focused block around the femoral nerve.
- **Infra-inguinal fascia iliaca block:** the classic volume block below the inguinal ligament.
- **Supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block:** the same plane, approached higher, with more reliable spread.
- **PENG block:** a newer block aimed at the nerves to the front of the hip capsule.

## The evidence in brief

A Cochrane review of peripheral nerve blocks for hip fracture found:

- Less pain on movement within 30 minutes of the block.
- A lower risk of acute confusion.
- A lower risk of chest infection.
- Less time to first mobilisation after surgery.

The certainty varies between outcomes, but the direction is consistent. A block is a cheap, low-risk intervention with clear benefit for a vulnerable group.

## The nerve supply of the hip

The hip joint receives branches from the **femoral**, **obturator** and **accessory obturator** nerves at the front, and from the sciatic nerve and nerve to quadratus femoris at the back. The skin over the outer thigh and the site of a lateral surgical incision belongs to the **lateral femoral cutaneous nerve**.

No single block covers all of these. Each block in this module reaches a different combination, and that shapes the choice.

## What this module is, and is not

This module teaches the theory: the safety system, the anatomy on the screen, the needle path and the evidence. It does not make you competent to perform a block. Hands-on skill comes from supervised practice under your trust's governance.

> **Before your first block** Check your trust's hip fracture block pathway, its training requirements and its sign-off process.

## Who should not have a block

- A patient who declines, or who lacks capacity and does not need a block in their best interests.
- Infection at the injection site.
- Allergy to local anaesthetic.
- Previous femoral bypass surgery at the site, unless an expert agrees.
- Anticoagulation is a relative contraindication. Superficial, compressible blocks such as the fascia iliaca block are often acceptable. Follow your trust's guidance.
